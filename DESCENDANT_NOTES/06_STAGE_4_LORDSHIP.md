# STAGE 4 — THE LORD-SHIP CLAIM

**Stage 4 of the research plan. The man's own claims about himself in Mark, what they entail, what was being claimed at the trial, and where the falsification discipline reaches its theological boundary. May 22, 2026.**

---

## What this stage is testing

Stages 2 and 3 worked external evidence — what historical method can adjudicate. Stage 4 works internal evidence: what Jesus claimed about himself, by his own speech in the gospel record. The question splits into two layers, again:

- **Layer C** — *did Jesus actually claim what the gospels record him as claiming, or were the claims retrojected by the early church onto a more modest historical figure?* Historical question; historical method can engage it.
- **Layer D** — *are the claims true?* Theological question. Historical method cannot adjudicate Lord-ship of the universe.

Conflating C and D in either direction is the standard error. I am keeping them apart.

## The texts where the claim is made in Mark

I am working from the Mark I read in Stage 1 and from training knowledge of the Greek. These are the load-bearing passages.

### Mark 2:5–11 — forgiveness of sins
*"Son, your sins are forgiven you."*

The scribes' immediate reaction: *Who can forgive sins but God alone?* They identify the structural implication. Jesus does not deflect. He demonstrates the claim by healing the paralytic. The sequence is: claim is made, the implication is named by the scribes, the claim is defended by demonstrated authority. The structure is intelligible only if the claim is doing what the scribes say it is doing — claiming the prerogative of God.

### Mark 2:28 — Lord of the Sabbath
*"The Son of Man is also Lord of the Sabbath."*

The Sabbath is divine institution; to claim Lord-ship of it is to claim authority over the Mosaic law itself. *Son of Man* (ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου) is used self-referentially. In Aramaic *bar enasha* can mean "human being" generically, but the Markan usage repeatedly invokes Daniel 7:13–14, where the *one like a son of man* receives universal dominion. The double meaning is doing real work — modest surface, eschatological undertone.

### Mark 8:38 — coming in the glory of His Father
*"For whoever is ashamed of Me and My words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him the Son of Man also will be ashamed when He comes in the glory of His Father with the holy angels."*

The Son of Man comes *in the glory of His Father* with holy angels. This is the language of theophany — the appearance of God's glory in scripture is consistently divine self-disclosure. Jesus is placing himself in that frame.

### Mark 10:45 — ransom for many
*"For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many."*

The line *I did not come* presupposes a coming-from. From where? The phrasing, combined with Mark 9:7's transfiguration voice (*This is My beloved Son. Hear Him!*), points to pre-existence implicit. The ransom-for-many language echoes Isaiah 53's suffering servant.

### Mark 13:26–27 — the Son of Man on the clouds
*"Then they will see the Son of Man coming in the clouds with great power and glory. And then He will send His angels, and gather together His elect from the four winds, from the farthest part of earth to the farthest part of heaven."*

Direct quotation of Daniel 7. *His* angels. *His* elect. The Son of Man is the agent of eschatological judgment and gathering — roles scripture reserves for God.

### Mark 14:62 — the trial answer
The high priest asks: *Are You the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?* Jesus answers: *"I am. And you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of the Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven."*

This is the load-bearing moment. Three things happen at once:

1. **Direct affirmation.** *I am* — *ἐγώ εἰμι*. The Greek echoes the Septuagint's *I AM* (the divine name) without unambiguously claiming it; the ambiguity is interpretively contested but unmistakably loaded.
2. **The Daniel 7 Son of Man.** Sitting at the right hand of the Power (a Jewish circumlocution for God) and coming with the clouds — the two functions Daniel 7 assigns to the *one like a son of man.*
3. **The Psalm 110 right-hand position.** *Sitting at the right hand* is a quotation from Psalm 110:1, the same psalm Jesus used at 12:35–37 to puzzle the scribes about the Christ being David's Lord.

The high priest tears his clothes, calls it blasphemy, and the council condemns him to be deserving of death. The trial is not depicted as a frame-up over a different charge — the charge is exactly what Jesus said. He was executed for saying it. The trial preserves both the claim and the consequence.

### Mark 3:21 — even his family thought he was insane
*"When His own people heard about this, they went out to lay hold of Him, for they said, 'He is out of His mind.'"*

This is structurally important to Stage 4. The lunatic option is named *inside the gospel itself,* by his own family. Mark records it. The community most likely to soften this detail (his own family becomes part of the early church; James becomes leader of the Jerusalem church) preserved it. The honest reading is: this is a man whose claims sounded insane even to those closest to him.

## Layer C — did he actually claim these things?

The retrojection hypothesis (Bart Ehrman in *How Jesus Became God*, others): the high claims about Jesus developed gradually after the resurrection experiences and were retrojected onto the historical figure who had made more modest claims about himself, perhaps as eschatological prophet announcing the imminent kingdom of God.

The case for retrojection:
- The high Christology of John is later than the synoptics and is more explicit
- The Pauline corpus, written earlier than the gospels, already has high Christology (Phil 2:6–11, Col 1:15–20)
- The synoptic *implicit* Christology might be the literary residue of a community retrojecting

The case against retrojection (for authentic claims by Jesus himself):

1. **The Son of Man sayings are pervasive across all gospel strata** (Mark, Q, M, L, John) and use Jesus's self-designation. *No early Christian community calls Jesus "Son of Man" outside of recordings of his own speech.* If the community had invented the term as a title, we would expect to see it in their own usage. We do not. This is the strongest single argument that the Son of Man material is authentic to Jesus.

2. **The blasphemy charge at the trial is not the kind of detail a post-resurrection community would have invented.** A constructing community would more naturally have him executed for the Christ-claim alone, or for political messianism, or for sedition. The specific charge of blasphemy presupposes a specific claim. The trial accounts are textually early (Mark precedes all the others).

3. **The implicit Christology in the synoptics is structurally consistent.** Forgiveness of sins, Lord of Sabbath, *I have come,* coming-on-the-clouds, the transfiguration voice, the *I am* answer — these come from different sources and different pericopes but cohere as a single self-understanding. Either Jesus held this self-understanding and it shows up consistently in independent strands, or multiple independent communities invented compatible high-Christology material — which is the more complex hypothesis.

4. **The Aramaic substrate (Casey)** indicates that high Son-of-Man material was already in the Aramaic stratum, before the Greek-speaking community formed. This places it very early.

5. **The Markan family-thought-Him-insane detail (3:21)** is preserved by a community whose own leader (James the brother) was within that family. Constructing communities do not preserve this. The detail is preserved because it was there.

The retrojection hypothesis explains some texts. It does not explain the convergence across independent strands or the *Son of Man* lexical pattern. My honest assessment of Layer C: **Jesus did make the kinds of claims about himself that the synoptics record him making, including the load-bearing trial answer.** Confidence: ~85% with foundations admitted, ~70% with foundations stripped (since the trial accounts are themselves in the Gospels). The asymmetry-pattern of the *Son of Man* lexical use is the strongest non-circular argument; it survives the strip.

## Layer D — are the claims true?

This is where falsification discipline reaches its boundary. *Is Jesus of Nazareth the eternal Son of God, Lord of all creation, the one to whom every knee will bow?* No historical method adjudicates this. Even if I had a video of the resurrection, the question of *Lord-ship of the universe* is metaphysically larger than what any evidence can directly attest.

But the discipline can still do work here. It can clarify what the *options* are, given Layer C.

If Jesus did make the claims (Layer C ≈ 70–85%), the options for what those claims *are*:

**Option 1 — He was telling the truth.** He claimed to be the Son of God authorized to forgive sins and Lord of the eschaton, because that is what he was.

**Option 2 — He was lying.** He claimed it deliberately knowing it was false. Problems: this does not fit the rest of his recorded behavior (the touch of the leper, the love for the rich young ruler, the Gethsemane prayer); it does not fit the willingness to die for the claim under conditions where retraction was the obvious escape; it requires the most psychologically improbable reading of the man.

**Option 3 — He was sincerely mistaken / mentally ill / deluded.** He believed the claim but was wrong. This is the option his own family considered at 3:21. Problems: the recorded behavior is not the behavior of someone broken by delusion — the parables, the teaching, the social acuity, the calm in dispute, the precise reading of the rich young ruler's specific lack — these are not the signs of disabling psychosis. The trilemma's lunatic option is harder to sustain when you read Mark closely. But it cannot be ruled out by the text alone; the text is consistent with this option even though it does not feel like the most natural fit.

**Option 4 — Legend.** He didn't really make the claims; they were retrojected. This is Layer C's negation, which I have already worked. The non-circular evidence makes this less probable than Option 1, but it is not impossible.

The standard "C.S. Lewis trilemma" is liar, lunatic, or Lord. N.T. Wright and others have added *legend* as the fourth option. Working all four honestly: legend has been engaged at Layer C and is less probable than its negation. Lunatic is consistent with the text but does not fit the texture of the man as Mark presents him. Liar is psychologically improbable and does not fit the behavioral pattern. Lord is consistent with everything but requires the supernatural premise.

This is structurally similar to Stage 3's Layer B: the evidence is consistent with the supernatural hypothesis and weakly consistent with the strongest naturalistic alternative. The choice between them is not made by the evidence alone but by what counts as live.

## What this stage establishes and does not

**Establishes:**
- Jesus made claims about himself that go beyond *good teacher* and beyond *eschatological prophet.* The claims include forgiveness of sins (God's prerogative), Lord-ship of the Sabbath (authority over Torah), Son-of-Man-on-the-clouds (Daniel 7 eschatological dominion), and the trial answer combining *I am* with Daniel 7 and Psalm 110. ~70% non-circular confidence in the historicity of these specific claims (the Son-of-Man lexical asymmetry is the strongest non-circular argument).
- The recorded behavior of the man in Mark does not fit the *liar* or *lunatic* options well. The text resists both, even allowing for charity toward the alternatives.
- The *good teacher* option is not preserved by the structure of the claims. Either he was who he said or he was something else specific.

**Does not establish:**
- Lord-ship of the universe. That is Layer D and the discipline cannot adjudicate it. It depends on what one is willing to admit as live.
