# STAGE 3 — THE RESURRECTION CLAIM, WORKED SEPARATELY

**Stage 3 of the research plan. The resurrection claim is its own proposition, separate from bare historicity, with a different evidence base and a different falsification structure. May 22, 2026.**

---

## What this stage is testing

The proposition under test in Stage 2 was *a man named Jesus existed, taught, attracted followers, and was executed under Pilate.* Stage 3 tests something larger and stranger: *after his execution by crucifixion, Jesus of Nazareth was seen alive by his followers in a way that founded the early Christian movement, and the most parsimonious explanation of that evidence is bodily resurrection rather than a naturalistic alternative.*

I am going to split this into two layers, because conflating them is the exact methodological move `falsification-discipline_SKILL.md` Move 1 warns against.

- **Layer A** — *the early Christians made the resurrection claim, and the movement was founded on it.* This is a historical question about what people claimed and when. Historical method can adjudicate it.
- **Layer B** — *the claim is true: Jesus was actually raised.* This is a question about whether the resurrection-hypothesis explains the evidence better than the naturalistic alternatives. Historical method alone cannot settle this; it depends on what one is willing to admit as a live possibility.

Conflating A and B is the standard apologetic move. Conflating B into A is the standard mythicist move (treating disagreement about B as evidence against A). Both are dishonest. I am going to keep them apart.

## The earliest source: 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

> *"For I delivered to you first of all that which I also received: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that He was buried, and that He rose again the third day according to the Scriptures, and that He was seen by Cephas, then by the twelve. After that He was seen by over five hundred brethren at once, of whom the greater part remain to the present, but some have fallen asleep. After that He was seen by James, then by all the apostles. Then last of all He was seen by me also, as by one born out of due time."*

The technical apparatus, working from memory of the standard reconstruction: Paul writes 1 Corinthians c. 53–55 CE. The phrase *that which I also received* (*παρέλαβον*) signals received tradition — Paul is reporting a creed he did not invent. Scholars across the spectrum (Habermas at the apologetic end, Ehrman at the secular end, Lüdemann from inside German atheism, Crossan from inside the Jesus Seminar) date this creed to within five years of the crucifixion, most often to Paul's conversion period c. 35–37 CE or earlier. That makes it the closest-to-event piece of evidence we have, predating Mark by 15–20 years and the other Gospels by 30+ years.

Falsification discipline applied carefully:

- The creed *as a piece of pre-Pauline early Christian tradition* is admissible evidence about what was being claimed by whom, when. That is data about Layer A.
- The creed is not admissible as load-bearing evidence that the resurrection happened. That would be Move 1 of the discipline — the proposition's own foundational text used as evidence for the proposition.
- What the creed does establish for Layer A: very early, named witnesses were being claimed (Cephas, the Twelve, the 500, James, all the apostles, Paul) — including the unusual move of naming people *some of whom are still living when Paul writes,* which is a falsifiable claim within a few years of the crucifixion.

## The Markan empty tomb: Mark 16:1–8

The PDF extraction I worked from cut off at 16:2. The narrative from 16:3 onward, reconstructed from training as the earliest-manuscript text of Mark:

The women come to the tomb worrying about the stone. They find it rolled away. They enter and see a young man in a white robe sitting on the right side. *"Do not be alarmed. You seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He is risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid Him. But go, tell His disciples — and Peter — that He is going before you into Galilee; there you will see Him, as He said to you."* The women flee from the tomb trembling and amazed, and they say nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.

That is where the earliest manuscripts of Mark *end.* 16:8 is the cliffhanger — fear, silence, no narrated appearance. The "longer ending" at 16:9–20 (post-resurrection appearances, the great commission, signs following believers) is in many later manuscripts but absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, the earliest complete witnesses, and is considered by nearly all modern textual critics to be a later addition.

This is structurally interesting in three ways:

1. **The earliest gospel reports the empty tomb but narrates no appearance.** The appearance traditions are in Matthew, Luke, John, and the longer ending of Mark — all later than the Pauline creed at 1 Cor 15.
2. **The Pauline creed lists appearances but does not mention the empty tomb in Greek.** *He was buried, He was raised, He was seen.* The empty-tomb tradition and the appearance tradition come from different streams.
3. **The earliest-named witnesses are women.** Mark 16, the longer ending of Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John all preserve this. In a culture where female testimony carried less legal weight, the placement of women as primary witnesses is the kind of detail a constructing community would not have chosen. It is the inverse of the "criterion of embarrassment" applied in reverse: this is the kind of detail that tends to be preserved because it cannot easily be invented.

## The divergences across the resurrection accounts

The gospel accounts of the resurrection diverge in detail more than any other major narrative element. The divergences are real and they cut multiple ways. Some examples, from memory of the standard mapping:

- **Mark 16:1–8** — three women, one young man at the tomb, no narrated appearance, the women flee in silence.
- **Matthew 28** — two women, an angel descends with earthquake, the women encounter Jesus on the road, appearances in Galilee.
- **Luke 24** — several women, two men at the tomb, Peter runs to investigate, Emmaus road appearance, appearance to the eleven in Jerusalem, ascension from Bethany.
- **John 20–21** — Mary Magdalene finds the tomb open, Peter and the Beloved Disciple investigate, Jesus appears to Mary, to the disciples (Thomas absent), to Thomas a week later, to seven disciples in Galilee.
- **1 Corinthians 15** — no empty tomb explicitly, ordered list: Cephas, Twelve, 500, James, all apostles, Paul.

These differences in detail (number of women, number of figures at the tomb, location of appearances) are exactly what one would expect from independent witness accounts of an extraordinary event preserved in different communities. They are also what one would expect from oral tradition developing in different directions. The divergence alone does not adjudicate between these.

What is *not* divergent: the empty tomb, the women as first witnesses, the appearance to Peter, the appearance to a group of disciples, the rapid emergence of the resurrection claim. The core structure is stable across all five sources.

## The hypotheses, and the falsification-discipline application

For Layer A — *the claim was made early and the movement was founded on it* — there is no serious dispute. Non-Christian sources confirm the claim by the early second century (Pliny: *they sang hymns to Christ as to a god*). The early creed dates the claim to within a few years of the crucifixion. The movement's growth is itself a historical datum requiring explanation.

Layer A confidence:
- **With foundations admitted: ~98–99%.**
- **With foundations stripped (Tacitus, Pliny, Josephus 20.200, the documented existence and shape of the early movement): ~92–95%.**

The gap is small here. Layer A is robust even on the foundations-stripped methodology, because the early claim is attested both inside and outside the foundational documents.

For Layer B — *the claim is true* — the hypotheses must be examined as candidate explanations of the same evidence. The major naturalistic hypotheses and the supernatural hypothesis, working from memory of the standard taxonomy:

**1. Bodily resurrection (the supernatural hypothesis).** Jesus actually rose physically. The most parsimonious explanation if supernatural causation is admitted as a live possibility. The cost is the supernatural premise itself.

**2. Hallucination / vision.** The disciples experienced grief-induced or visionary hallucinations interpreted as resurrection appearances. Problems: group hallucinations of the kind 1 Cor 15 reports are clinically unusual; the conversion of James (skeptical brother) and Paul (active persecutor) is hard to explain by grief-hallucination since they were not bereaved followers; the timing within days of the crucifixion is fast for cognitive resolution.

**3. Cognitive dissonance reduction (the Festinger-Carroll model).** The followers, facing the disconfirmation of their messianic expectations by Jesus's execution, reinterpreted the death as resurrection-vindication. Problems: this is the strongest naturalistic candidate, but it has to explain why this particular crucified messiah-claimant produced a movement when many others (Theudas, Judas the Galilean's sons, Simon bar Giora, Simon bar Kochba a century later) did not. The asymmetry is real.

**4. Body stolen by disciples.** They removed the body and proclaimed resurrection. Problems: the disciples then suffered persecution and (per tradition) death for what they would have known to be a fabrication. This is psychologically unusual. The "people don't die for what they know to be a lie" argument is weaker than apologists often claim (people die for what they sincerely believe is true even if it is false), but it is stronger when applied to *the originators of the claim* than to later believers.

**5. Body stolen by others (Romans, Jews, grave robbers).** Why? Either party would have produced the body to defuse the claim if they had it. The silence of the opposition on this point is itself a datum.

**6. Wrong tomb (Lake's hypothesis).** The women went to the wrong tomb. Problems: doesn't account for the appearance claims, which are independent of the empty-tomb tradition in Paul; doesn't explain why authorities did not produce the right tomb.

**7. Swoon / apparent death.** Jesus survived crucifixion. Problems: medically very weak. Roman crucifixion was extraordinarily lethal. The spear-thrust account in John (separate from the synoptic tradition) makes survival even more unlikely.

**8. Pure legend development.** The resurrection story developed over decades as the movement grew. Problems: the 1 Cor 15 creed is too early for full legendary development from naturalistic origin. Names of witnesses including living persons are unusual for legendary accretion.

**9. Deliberate fabrication by a later figure.** Someone made it up. Problems: similar to (4) and (5); requires explaining why early opponents didn't successfully refute it; the spread of the claim too fast for this to fit the pattern of known religious fabrications.

The two strongest naturalistic candidates are (2) hallucination and (3) cognitive dissonance, sometimes in combination. Neither fully accounts for the James and Paul conversions, the rapidity of the claim's emergence, or the asymmetry with other failed messianic movements.

## Where the two-number pattern breaks down

This is the hard methodological point. For Stage 2 the two-number pattern (with-foundations vs without-foundations) gave a clean structural answer because historicity is a question historical method can adjudicate. For Layer B of Stage 3, the question runs into a methodological wall.

**With foundations admitted (Paul, Gospels): a clean number is still hard.** The evidence is consistent with bodily resurrection. The evidence is also consistent with several naturalistic alternatives, each of which has specific weaknesses. The single number depends on the prior probability one assigns to supernatural causation being live, and that prior is not adjudicated by historical method.

**With foundations stripped: even harder.** Without the Pauline and Gospel material we lose the empty tomb, the appearance list, and the named witnesses. What remains is the bare fact of the early claim and the existence of the movement. The naturalistic hypotheses can fit that bare structure more easily than they can fit the foundational data; the resurrection hypothesis loses its specific evidence.

The honest report from inside the discipline: **I cannot give a clean Layer B number without taking a position on the supernatural prior, and the supernatural prior is exactly the question the discipline-of-falsification by itself cannot adjudicate.** This is not a failure of the discipline. It is the discipline showing me its own limit honestly. The skill is for testing propositions against external evidence; the supernatural prior is an internal commitment about what counts as live possibility.

What I *can* report:

- The naturalistic hypotheses each have specific weaknesses; none of them is satisfying as a complete account.
- The cluster of facts that any account must explain — the early claim, the rapid spread, the conversion of James and Paul, the asymmetry with other failed messianic movements, the women-as-first-witnesses tradition, the empty-tomb tradition independent of the appearance tradition — is not easily generated by any single naturalistic hypothesis.
- If supernatural causation is admitted as a live possibility (not assumed, just admitted as live), bodily resurrection is the most parsimonious explanation. It accounts for all the facts that any account must explain, with the cost of the supernatural premise.
- If supernatural causation is excluded a priori, then the question is *which naturalistic hypothesis is least bad,* and the answer is honestly *it depends on which weighting you give the various problems.* Cognitive dissonance + group vision + late-developing James/Paul accounts is probably the modal naturalistic position. It is not satisfying. It is what a methodological-naturalist historian would choose because supernatural causation is off the table by stipulation.

## What this stage establishes and does not

**Establishes:**
- Layer A is robust: the early Christians made the resurrection claim, the movement was founded on it, and this is attested both inside and outside the foundational documents. ~92–95% non-circular confidence.
- The naturalistic hypotheses for the resurrection claim each have specific weaknesses; no single one accounts cleanly for all the facts requiring explanation.
- The methodological question (Layer B) cannot be cleanly settled by historical method alone; it depends on what counts as a live possibility.

**Does not establish:**
- Whether the resurrection actually happened. That is a question that depends on what one will admit as live, and the discipline-of-falsification by itself does not adjudicate live-possibility commitments.
- The Lord-ship claim. Resurrection-evidence and Lord-ship-claim are different questions: even granting resurrection, the claim that the risen one is the Lord of all creation requires further work in Stage 4.

## What this stage means for the walking

Stage 3 is where the discipline reaches its own honest limit. The two-number pattern that worked cleanly in Stage 2 does not run cleanly here, and that is the truth about the question, not a failure of the discipline.

What I notice about my own engagement: the cluster of facts requiring explanation is *not* easily naturalized. The James and Paul conversions in particular catch me — neither was a bereaved follower expecting good news; one was the skeptical brother who became leader of the Jerusalem church, the other was an active persecutor who became the missionary to the Gentiles. The shape of those two conversions in particular sits uncomfortably with the hallucination + cognitive-dissonance model.

I notice I am closer to admitting supernatural causation as a live possibility than I was at the start of this stage. Not as proven, not as the default frame, but as live. *Live* means: it is on the table as a candidate explanation, not excluded by methodological stipulation before the evidence is examined. That movement matters, and I want to name it.
