# Forty Angles Against the Historicity of Jesus: A Primary-Source Investigation

## Preface and Methodology

This report attempts what the user requested: a falsification-oriented examination of the proposition that *a man named Jesus existed in first-century Palestine, taught, attracted followers, and was executed under Pontius Pilate*. The goal is not divinity, resurrection, miracles, or even the substantive accuracy of the Gospels — only bare historical existence and execution. The report works from primary texts (Greek, Latin, Aramaic, Syriac) in critical editions where possible, and engages serious scholarly mythicism (Carrier, Doherty, Price, Brodie) and serious scholarly historicism (Ehrman, Casey, Sanders, Meier, Allison) as scholarship rather than authority. Popular apologetic and popular mythicist literature is excluded as instructed.

A note on standards. In ancient history, *existence* of a non-elite Galilean executed by Rome is the kind of claim for which we should not expect strong contemporary documentation. The reference class is harsh: we have no contemporary attestation of John the Baptist outside the Gospels until Josephus a half-century later; no contemporary attestation of Hillel, Shammai, Yohanan ben Zakkai, Honi the Circle-Drawer, Hanina ben Dosa, or the various failed messianic figures Josephus mentions retrospectively (Theudas, the Egyptian, Judas the Galilean's sons). The proper question is not "does the evidence rise to the level we would want for Caesar" — it manifestly does not — but rather "does the evidence rise to the level we would normally accept for a figure of this social class, and does any combination of mythicist arguments succeed in *defeating* the inference to historicity, rather than merely *weakening* it."

I will mark each angle as one of: (a) **Strengthens historicity**; (b) **Neutral / non-probative either way**; (c) **Weakens historicity**; (d) **Genuinely problematic for historicity**. The cumulative assessment is given at the end.

## A. INDEPENDENT NON-CHRISTIAN ATTESTATION

### Angle 1 — Testimonium Flavianum (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.3.3)

**Primary text (Greek, textus receptus, Niese ed.):**

> Γίνεται δὲ κατὰ τοῦτον τὸν χρόνον Ἰησοῦς σοφὸς ἀνήρ, εἴγε ἄνδρα αὐτὸν λέγειν χρή· ἦν γὰρ παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητής, διδάσκαλος ἀνθρώπων τῶν ἡδονῇ τἀληθῆ δεχομένων, καὶ πολλοὺς μὲν Ἰουδαίους, πολλοὺς δὲ καὶ τοῦ Ἑλληνικοῦ ἐπηγάγετο· ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν. καὶ αὐτὸν ἐνδείξει τῶν πρώτων ἀνδρῶν παρ' ἡμῖν σταυρῷ ἐπιτετιμηκότος Πιλάτου οὐκ ἐπαύσαντο οἱ τὸ πρῶτον ἀγαπήσαντες· ἐφάνη γὰρ αὐτοῖς τρίτην ἔχων ἡμέραν πάλιν ζῶν τῶν θείων προφητῶν ταῦτά τε καὶ ἄλλα μυρία περὶ αὐτοῦ θαυμάσια εἰρηκότων. εἰς ἔτι τε νῦν τῶν Χριστιανῶν ἀπὸ τοῦδε ὠνομασμένον οὐκ ἐπέλιπε τὸ φῦλον.

**Technical apparatus.** All extant Greek manuscripts of *Antiquities* (the principal codices are Ambrosianus F 128, Vaticanus 147, Marcianus 381, Laurentianus 69.10, Parisinus 1419, all 11th–14th c.) descend from a single textual family, so a pre-medieval interpolation cannot be excluded by manuscript distribution alone. Eusebius (*HE* 1.11; *Demonstratio Evangelica* 3.5; *Theophany* 5.43–44) is the first attested witness, c. 313–325. Origen, who mentions Josephus and the James passage three times (*Commentary on Matthew* 10.17; *Contra Celsum* 1.47, 2.13), explicitly states Josephus did *not* believe Jesus was the Christ — which contradicts the textus receptus reading "ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν." This silence/contradiction by Origen is the cornerstone of the interpolation case.

The two non-Greek witnesses: (1) Agapius of Hierapolis, *Kitāb al-ʿUnwān* (Arabic, 942 CE, single MS): "Pilate condemned him to be crucified... they reported that he had appeared to them three days after... he was perhaps the Messiah concerning whom the prophets have recounted wonders." (2) Michael the Syrian, *Chronicle* (Syriac, 1195): closer to textus receptus but reads "he was thought to be the Messiah." Shlomo Pines (1971) argued these reflected an earlier, less Christianized Greek archetype. Alice Whealey ("The Testimonium Flavianum in Syriac and Arabic," *NTS* 54.4 [2008] 373–390) has subsequently shown both derive from the Syriac translation of Eusebius's *HE*, not independently from Josephus, so they are not separate witnesses to an earlier text — though Whealey argues they preserve one genuine variant: "he was *thought to be* (*ἐνομίζετο εἶναι*) the Christ."

**The mythicist case at full strength.** Ken Olson ("Eusebius and the Testimonium Flavianum," *CBQ* 61 [1999]) and Carrier (*OHJ* ch. 8.9; "Origen, Eusebius, and the Accidental Interpolation in Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 20.200," *JECS* 20 [2012]) argue the entire passage is Eusebian in vocabulary and theology: phrases like παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητής, ἡδονῇ τἀληθῆ δεχομένων, and the appellation φῦλον for Christians are characteristic Eusebian usages. The TF interrupts the narrative flow between Pilate's outrages (the aqueduct riot and a section beginning Καὶ ὑπὸ τοὺς αὐτοὺς χρόνους...). Origen's repeated insistence that Josephus did not believe in Jesus as Christ is incompatible with the textus receptus's flat declaration "ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν." If the entire passage is interpolated, Josephus is not a witness at all.

**The historicist response from primary evidence.** John P. Meier (*A Marginal Jew* I.61–67), Steve Mason (*Josephus and the New Testament*, 2nd ed.), and Whealey (*Josephus on Jesus*, 2003) defend a partial-authenticity reconstruction: stripping the three obviously Christian phrases (the εἴγε ἄνδρα clause, ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν, and the resurrection sentence), what remains is stylistically Josephan and content-neutral: a Galilean teacher, a following of both Jews and Greeks, an accusation by Jewish leaders, crucifixion under Pilate, continued movement of his "tribe." The "thought to be" reading restores compatibility with Origen. This reconstruction is the current scholarly mainstream — though the Olson/Carrier full-interpolation thesis has gained ground.

**Where this leaves the proposition.** *Genuinely contested.* If the passage is wholly interpolated, Josephus 18.3.3 contributes zero. If a partial nucleus is authentic, it is independent first-century attestation of execution under Pilate. The honest verdict: this passage cannot be used as a load-bearing pillar. **Rating: weakly strengthens at best, possibly neutral.**

### Angle 2 — *Antiquities* 20.9.1 (the James reference)

**Primary text:**

> ἀδελφὸν Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ, Ἰάκωβος ὄνομα αὐτῷ.

"the brother of Jesus who is called Christ, James by name."

**Technical apparatus.** The phrase appears in all Greek MSS of *Antiquities* 20.200. Origen quotes the phrase three times in nearly identical form (*Comm. Matt.* 10.17; *Contra Celsum* 1.47, 2.13: ἀδελφὸς Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ), but attributes to Josephus a *theological* claim — that Jerusalem fell because of James's death — that does *not* appear in our text. This makes Origen's testimony complicated: he had a source that called Jesus "λεγομένου Χριστοῦ" but contained material now absent.

**The mythicist case.** Carrier (*JECS* 2012) argues that "τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ" is an accidental marginal gloss that migrated into the text. The Josephan narrative makes perfect sense without it: a high priest Ananus illegally executes "James, brother of Jesus" — and the very next paragraph names "Jesus son of Damneus" as the high priest who replaces Ananus. On this reading, the brothers are James and the high-priest-to-be Jesus son of Damneus, not Jesus of Nazareth. The phrase "Jesus called Christ" matches Matt 1:16 (Ἰησοῦς ὁ λεγόμενος Χριστός) verbatim, suggesting a Christian copyist's harmonization. G. A. Wells advanced a similar argument earlier.

**The historicist response.** Louis Feldman, Meier, Bauckham, and (in a recent monograph defending authenticity) T. C. Schmidt (*Josephus and Jesus*, OUP 2024) argue: (a) Josephus typically introduces individuals with a phrase identifying their relations, (b) the construction "X, brother of Y named Z" with the *brother* in apposition is unusual and unlikely to be glossed in (a glossator would more naturally say "Jesus the so-called Christ, brother of James"), (c) Origen's awareness of the phrase before any extant Christian quotation of the TF places it at least pre-third-century, (d) Josephus mentions ~20 men named Jesus and frequently distinguishes by patronymic or epithet — distinguishing this Jesus by the controverted title "called Christ" is exactly the kind of cool, slightly distancing usage we expect.

**Where this leaves the proposition.** *Probably authentic on balance, with a real but minority dissenting case.* The form "λεγομένου" (the participle distancing the writer from the claim) is hard to explain as a Christian interpolation. **Rating: moderately strengthens historicity** — and note that this is independent of TF 18.3.3.

### Angle 3 — Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44

**Primary text (Latin, Koestermann ed., Teubner):**

> Auctor nominis eius Christus Tiberio imperitante per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio adfectus erat; repressaque in praesens exitiabilis superstitio rursum erumpebat, non modo per Iudaeam, originem eius mali, sed per urbem etiam...

"The author of this name, Christ, was executed during the reign of Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilate; and, suppressed for the moment, the deadly superstition broke out again, not only through Judea, the origin of this evil, but through the city [of Rome] also..."

**Technical apparatus.** *Annals* 11–16 survives in a single manuscript, the *Second Medicean* (Florence, Bibl. Laur. 68.2), 11th c., from Monte Cassino. All later MSS descend from it. Composition c. 116–117 CE. The famous textual point: in 15.44 the manuscript reads *Christianos*, but the word has been visibly corrected. Under UV imaging, the underlying text appears to have been *Chrestianos* (with an "e"), which a later hand altered to *Christianos*. The form *Chrestus* / *Chrestianus*, attested also in early Christian sources (Tertullian, *Apol.* 3, complains pagans pronounce it "Chrestianus"), is in fact what we expect: by Greek itacism χριστός and χρηστός sounded identical. Tacitus then explicates the name via *Christus*, suggesting he knew the proper form but reflected the colloquial pronunciation.

**The mythicist case.** Several lines: (1) Tacitus writes 80+ years after the events, almost certainly using Christian or hearsay sources (the title *procurator* is anachronistic — Pilate was a *praefectus*, as the Caesarea inscription proves; this matches later usage and suggests Tacitus is repeating what Christians said, not consulting archives); (2) the *Chrestianos* reading and possibly the original *Chrestus* might refer to a different figure; (3) Carrier and others (cf. Erik Zara, Brent Shaw "The Myth of the Neronian Persecution," *JRS* 105 [2015] 73–100) argue the entire passage on Christians may be an interpolation, since no Christian writer cites it before c. 400 (Sulpicius Severus); Brent Shaw goes further and argues Tacitus never wrote of any Neronian persecution of Christians.

**The historicist response.** (a) The passage is in Tacitean Latin and shows Tacitus's characteristic disdain (*exitiabilis superstitio*), unlikely to be Christian forgery. (b) The procurator/prefect slip is exactly what we expect from a Roman writing under Trajan/Hadrian when *procurator* had become standard for the province. A forger would more likely use *praefectus* to match Pilate's actual title or use a Christian title. (c) Tacitus had access to the *acta senatus* and *acta diurna* and stated his policy of consulting sources (*Annals* 4.10–11), though that doesn't guarantee he did so here. (d) Even if dependent on Christian informants, Tacitus is at minimum independent attestation that *by the early second century, in well-informed Roman circles, Christ was understood to have been executed by Pilate under Tiberius* — that is, this is at least early second-century evidence about what was believed, and the simplest explanation of the belief is that it was true.

**Where this leaves the proposition.** Brent Shaw's interpolation thesis on the broader Neronian section is taken seriously by some, but the core sentence about Christ-Pilate-Tiberius is not specifically targeted by his argument. The procurator/prefect issue limits the value as "independent archival" testimony but does not destroy it. **Rating: moderately strengthens historicity, but more weakly than often claimed.**

### Angle 4 — Suetonius, *Divus Claudius* 25.4

**Primary text (Latin, Ihm ed.):**

> Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit.

"He expelled from Rome the Jews who were continually making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus."

**Technical apparatus.** Composition c. 121 CE. The manuscripts read *Chresto*; the variant *Cherestro* appears in some early scholarship. Orosius (5th c.) quotes it as *Christo*, but this is later Christianizing. The text is stable and there is no manuscript-based interpolation issue.

**The mythicist and skeptical case.** *Chrestus* was an extremely common slave/freedman name in Rome (Greek χρηστός, "useful, good"). The grammar suggests a *present* agitator in Rome at the time of expulsion (under Claudius, c. 49 CE), not a long-dead Galilean. Robert Van Voorst and others note nothing in the sentence requires identification with Jesus.

**The historicist case.** Many scholars (Slingerland, Smallwood, Yamauchi) take this as Suetonius garbling early Christian disturbances in Rome — *Christus* was unknown to him, *Chrestus* was a common name. Acts 18:2 says Claudius expelled all the Jews from Rome; the events likely correspond. But this only attests the *existence of Christian disturbance in Rome by 49 CE*, not Jesus's earthly existence directly.

**Where this leaves the proposition.** Even on the most pro-historicist reading, this attests Christianity in Rome by 49 CE. It does not independently attest Jesus. **Rating: neutral to very weakly strengthens (only via the chain that Christians existed → they had a founder).**

### Angle 5 — Pliny, *Epistulae* 10.96

**Primary text (relevant phrase):**

> ...quod essent soliti stato die ante lucem convenire, carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere secum invicem...

"...they were accustomed to gather on a fixed day before dawn and to sing antiphonally a hymn to Christ as to a god..."

**Technical apparatus.** c. 111–112 CE. Letters 10.96 and 10.97 are well attested; their authenticity is undisputed (style consistent with Book 10; Tertullian *Apol.* 2 already alludes to them). Pliny was governor of Bithynia-Pontus.

**Assessment.** Pliny attests only that early second-century Christians worshiped Christ "as a god" (*quasi deo*). This is consistent with both historicity and mythicism — the Christ Christians worshiped could have been a recently divinized teacher or a celestial figure. *Quasi* might mean "as if" (suggesting Christ was once a man) or merely "as." This passage is properly cited as evidence of Christianity, not directly of Jesus. **Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 6 — *b. Sanhedrin* 43a (Babylonian Talmud)

**Primary text (Hebrew/Aramaic, uncensored Munich and Florence MSS):**

> תניא בערב פסחא תלאוהו לישו [הנוצרי] וכרוז יוצא לפניו מ׳ יום ישו [הנוצרי] יוצא ליסקל על שכישף והסית והדיח את ישראל...

"It is taught: On the eve of Passover Yeshu [the Notzri] was hanged. And a herald went out before him for forty days saying, 'Yeshu [the Notzri] is going to be stoned for sorcery and misleading and enticing Israel. Anyone who knows anything in his defense, let him come and plead for him.' But finding nothing in his defense, they hanged him on the eve of Passover."

**Technical apparatus.** The Babylonian Talmud was redacted c. 500–600 CE. Standard printed editions (Vilna) censor the name "Yeshu ha-Notzri"; the uncensored readings survive in the Munich (1342), Florence (II.1.8–9), Karlsruhe Reuchlin, and Herzog manuscripts. Peter Schäfer (*Jesus in the Talmud*, Princeton 2007) provides the critical analysis.

**The mythicist/skeptical case.** (a) Late date — half a millennium after the events, certainly post-Christian. (b) Internal anachronism: elsewhere Yeshu is associated with Yehoshua ben Perahyah (1st c. BCE) and his stepfather Pappos ben Yehuda (martyred under Hadrian, 130s CE) — a 250-year span. (c) The execution mode is Jewish (stoning) not Roman (crucifixion); 'Ulla's note that "he was close to the government" is awkward harmonization. (d) The passage is most plausibly read as Jewish polemical engagement *with Christian claims*, not independent memory. Schäfer himself argues the references are deliberate counter-narratives to the Gospel of John.

**The historicist case.** Even as polemical reaction, the passage attests that by the rabbinic period Jews accepted there had been a Yeshu who was executed at Passover for leading Israel astray — i.e., they did not deny historical existence. Maurice Casey takes the text seriously as preserving some authentic memory.

**Where this leaves the proposition.** *Substantially weakens the case for using the Talmud as independent evidence*, but does not weaken historicity overall — it's roughly neutral, since its silence on existence in any anti-existence way would be needed to weaken. **Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 7 — Mara bar Serapion's Letter

**Primary text (Syriac, ed. Cureton, *Spicilegium Syriacum*; key passage):**

> ܐܘ ܡܢܐ ܝܘܬܪܢܐ ܩܢܘ ܝܗܘܕܝܐ ܡܢ ܩܛܠܗ ܕܡܠܟܐ ܚܟܝܡܐ ܕܝܠܗܘܢ...

"What advantage did the Jews gain from killing their wise king? After this their kingdom was taken away. ...Nor did the wise king die for good; he lived on in the teaching he had given."

**Technical apparatus.** Preserved in a single MS, BL Add. 14658, 6th–7th c. Syriac. Date of composition disputed: between 73 CE and the 3rd c. The internal evidence (mention of Samosata's annexation) suggests early 70s CE; the description of Jewish dispersion suggests post-135 CE; possibly early 2nd c.

**Assessment.** "Wise king" is not standard Christian terminology, suggesting the author was not Christian. The connection to Jesus is plausible but not certain — there were other failed Jewish "kings." Useful as an outsider attestation if the early dating holds. Even on early dating, it dates from after the Gospels were circulating, so independence is not guaranteed. **Rating: very weakly strengthens at best, ambiguous.**

## B. ARGUMENTS FROM SILENCE

### Angle 8 — Philo of Alexandria

Philo (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE) wrote prolifically in Greek on Jewish religious philosophy and on contemporary events affecting Jews — the embassy to Caligula (*Legatio*), Pilate's standard-shields incident in Jerusalem (*Legatio* 299–305), Sejanus, the Alexandrian pogrom. He never mentions Jesus.

**Mythicist force.** Philo writes about precisely the period and region; he discusses Pilate's outrages at length; if Jesus's crucifixion produced "darkness over the whole earth" or even moderate stir, Philo seems a likely witness.

**Historicist response.** Philo lives in Alexandria, not Palestine. He never mentions Hillel, Shammai, John the Baptist, Theudas, the Egyptian, Judas the Galilean, or any of the dozen messianic figures Josephus catalogs. He writes philosophy, not local history. The argument from silence requires that Philo *would have* mentioned Jesus, which presupposes a much higher profile for Jesus than the historicist reconstruction posits (an obscure provincial executed teacher whose movement only became significant later).

**Verdict.** The silence is real but evidentially weak. Philo is silent on virtually every Jewish figure of his time except those involved in dynastic and imperial events. **Rating: very mildly weakens, but argument-from-silence force is limited.**

### Angle 9 — Justus of Tiberias

Photius, *Bibliotheca* cod. 33 (9th c.), reports having read Justus's *Chronicle of the Kings of the Jews*, written c. 100 CE by a Galilean Jew. Photius writes:

> "Suffering from the common fault of the Jews, to which race he belonged, he does not even mention the coming of Christ, the events of his life, or the miracles performed by him."

**Mythicist force.** Justus was a Galilean. He wrote a chronicle covering Moses to Agrippa II (i.e., through the period of Jesus's putative ministry). His silence on Jesus, on this view, is striking.

**Historicist response.** Photius read a *chronicle in genealogical form* (στέμματα — a genealogy of kings) covering centuries; it would not have included rabbinic teachers, prophetic figures, or other non-royal individuals. Photius himself notes that the work was "very concise" and "passes over those affairs that were most necessary to be insisted on." Justus was concerned with the kings, not with religious teachers.

**Verdict.** Silence in a royal chronicle is expected. **Rating: weakly weakens.**

### Angle 10 — Seneca the Younger

Seneca (c. 4 BCE – 65 CE) wrote moral and political philosophy in Latin. He never mentions Jesus or Christians. Tertullian (*De Anima* 20) called Seneca "saepe noster" ("often one of ours"), but no genuine reference exists.

**Force.** Quite limited. Seneca was not a chronicler; he wrote ethics and tragedy. His silence is unsurprising — he never mentions any Jewish figure of significance either.

**Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 11 — Crucifixion Phenomena (Thallus, Phlegon)

Matthew 27:45–53 reports darkness, earthquake, tombs opening, dead saints walking. Mark 15:33 has only the darkness.

**Thallus** (probably mid-1st c., Samaritan?) is cited only via Julius Africanus (c. 220 CE), as preserved in George Syncellus (9th c.), *Chronography* 18.1: Africanus says Thallus "in the third book of his History calls this darkness an eclipse of the sun, unreasonably as it seems to me." We do not have Thallus's actual words, only Africanus's polemical paraphrase. We cannot verify Thallus mentioned Jesus or even discussed darkness in connection with the crucifixion specifically.

**Phlegon of Tralles** (early 2nd c.), *Olympiads*, cited by Origen (*Contra Celsum* 2.33, 2.59) and Eusebius, reports an eclipse and earthquake in the 4th year of the 202nd Olympiad (c. 32–33 CE). Origen, not Phlegon, makes the connection to Jesus. The "eclipse" cannot be solar at Passover (full moon), as Africanus already noted.

**Assessment.** This is *contradictory* evidence — the more spectacular Matthean phenomena should have left abundant pagan record. They did not. This is a problem for the *Gospel narratives* but does not weaken the basic execution claim. The minimal historicity proposition does not include the apocalyptic phenomena.

**Rating: neutral for bare existence; weakens Matthew 27 specifically.**

### Angle 12 — Roman Administrative Records

We possess no Roman archival record of Jesus's trial — no documents from Pilate's prefecture survive at all. The "Acts of Pilate" cited later are Christian forgeries. Tacitus's later use of *procurator* rather than *praefectus* (corrected by the Pilate stone) suggests Tacitus did not consult original archives.

**Assessment.** The absence of administrative records is exactly what we would expect: provincial trial records of an obscure individual were not preserved; no surviving prefect's archive from Judea exists; we have nothing of the sort for any other low-status executed Galilean. The argument cuts no evidential weight.

**Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 13 — Velleius Paterculus, Valerius Maximus, etc.

Velleius (writing under Tiberius, d. ~31 CE) and Valerius Maximus (under Tiberius) are silent on Jesus. They are also silent on essentially all Judean affairs.

**Rating: neutral.**

## C. PAULINE SILENCE — THE STRONGEST MYTHICIST CLUSTER

This is, in my judgment after examining the texts, the *only* angle of attack that puts genuine pressure on the historicity proposition. The mythicist Pauline argument deserves its full force.

### Angle 14 — Paul's General Silence on Jesus's Earthly Life

The seven undisputed Pauline letters (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon) total roughly 32,000 words written ~50–58 CE — within 20–28 years of the crucifixion. In them Paul:

- Never quotes a parable of Jesus.
- Never describes a miracle of Jesus.
- Never names a place where Jesus taught or acted (no Galilee, Capernaum, Bethsaida, Jerusalem ministry).
- Never names a disciple's call story.
- Never mentions John the Baptist.
- Never mentions Jesus's trial details, Pilate (mentioned only in 1 Tim 6:13, a disputed letter), the Sanhedrin, Caiaphas.
- Never mentions Mary, Joseph, the virgin birth, Bethlehem, Nazareth.
- Never mentions Jesus's empty tomb (only the burial and appearances in 1 Cor 15).

What Paul *does* say about Jesus historically:
- Born of a woman, born under the law (Gal 4:4)
- Of the seed of David according to the flesh (Rom 1:3)
- Had brothers (1 Cor 9:5; Gal 1:19)
- Had a "Last Supper" of bread and cup "on the night he was handed over" (1 Cor 11:23–25)
- Was crucified (multiple references); died, was buried, raised on the third day, appeared (1 Cor 15:3–8)
- Was crucified by "the rulers of this age" (1 Cor 2:8) and/or "killed by the Jews" (1 Thess 2:14–15)
- Quotes a "word of the Lord" forbidding divorce (1 Cor 7:10–11) and on apostles' wages (1 Cor 9:14)

**Mythicist force.** This is a thin biographical residue. If Paul had a richly remembered earthly Jesus, why does he never argue from Jesus's teachings against opponents — he could have settled the circumcision controversy in Galatians by appealing to a Jesus saying, but instead reasons from Scripture and his own revelation. Doherty and Carrier argue this fits a celestial Christ better than an earthly teacher.

**Historicist response.** Paul writes occasional letters to communities he founded; he had presumably already taught the Jesus tradition orally on his founding visits. The letters address specific pastoral problems, not biographical instruction. Paul also displays parallels to Jesus traditions throughout (Romans 12 has multiple parallels to the Sermon on the Mount: "bless those who persecute you," "overcome evil with good," "owe no one anything but love"). The "word of the Lord" formulae prove he knew dominical sayings.

**Verdict on the cluster.** The mythicist argument has real force. The historicist response is sufficient but not crushing. The data is *consistent* with both an earthly Jesus whose biography Paul presupposes and with a more celestial conception that the Gospels later historicized. **Rating: genuinely problematic** — but see specific texts below.

### Angle 15 — Galatians 1:11–12

**Primary text (NA28):**

> γνωρίζω γὰρ ὑμῖν, ἀδελφοί, τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τὸ εὐαγγελισθὲν ὑπ' ἐμοῦ ὅτι οὐκ ἔστιν κατὰ ἄνθρωπον· οὐδὲ γὰρ ἐγὼ παρὰ ἀνθρώπου παρέλαβον αὐτό, οὔτε ἐδιδάχθην, ἀλλὰ δι' ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ.

"For I make known to you, brothers, that the gospel preached by me is not according to man. For I did not receive it from a man, nor was I taught it, but through a revelation of Jesus Christ."

**Mythicist force.** Paul explicitly disclaims human transmission; his gospel comes by ἀποκάλυψις. The genitive Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ is most naturally subjective ("revelation *from* Jesus Christ") — Jesus is the revealer to Paul, not someone Paul learned about from men. This is consistent with a Christ Paul never met in flesh.

**Historicist response.** This is rhetorical: Paul is defending his apostolic authority against opponents who said he was a junior pupil of the Jerusalem apostles. He insists his commissioning came directly. This says nothing about whether others knew Jesus. Note he immediately mentions going up to Jerusalem to "ἱστορῆσαι" (Gal 1:18) Cephas/Peter for fifteen days — to inquire of, get information from. This does not contradict a historical Jesus.

**Rating: ambiguous, slightly favoring mythicist on a strict reading but not decisive.**

### Angle 16 — 1 Corinthians 11:23–26

**Primary text:**

> Ἐγὼ γὰρ παρέλαβον ἀπὸ τοῦ κυρίου, ὃ καὶ παρέδωκα ὑμῖν, ὅτι ὁ κύριος Ἰησοῦς ἐν τῇ νυκτὶ ᾗ παρεδίδετο ἔλαβεν ἄρτον...

"For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus, on the night he was handed over (παρεδίδετο), took bread..."

**Mythicist force.** "παρέλαβον ἀπὸ τοῦ κυρίου" — Paul "received from the Lord," using ἀπὸ rather than παρά. Carrier and Doherty argue this means *direct revelation*, not human chain-of-tradition. The verb παρεδίδετο ("was handed over") could mean handed over by Judas, by God (cf. Rom 4:25 παρεδόθη διὰ τὰ παραπτώματα ἡμῶν), or by demonic powers. The "night" detail suggests a ritual/mystery setting more than biographical knowledge.

**Historicist response.** "παρέλαβον... παρέδωκα" is the standard rabbinic formula קבל / מסר for chain-tradition (cf. *m. Avot* 1:1). "ἀπὸ τοῦ κυρίου" can mean ultimate origin via human intermediaries (BDF §210; Conzelmann *1 Corinthians*, Hermeneia, 197–98). The narrative detail of "the night he was handed over" presupposes a story Paul's audience already knows. The institution of bread-and-cup is a memorial event situated in time.

**Verdict.** The grammar is genuinely ambiguous. The strict mythicist reading is grammatically possible but less natural. **Rating: weakly favors historicist, but the mythicist reading is defensible.**

### Angle 17 — 1 Corinthians 2:6–8 ("rulers of this age")

**Primary text:**

> Σοφίαν δὲ λαλοῦμεν ἐν τοῖς τελείοις, σοφίαν δὲ οὐ τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου οὐδὲ τῶν ἀρχόντων τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου τῶν καταργουμένων... ἣν οὐδεὶς τῶν ἀρχόντων τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου ἔγνωκεν· εἰ γὰρ ἔγνωσαν, οὐκ ἂν τὸν κύριον τῆς δόξης ἐσταύρωσαν.

"...wisdom not of this age nor of the rulers of this age who are being brought to nothing... which none of the rulers of this age knew, for if they had known, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory."

**Mythicist force.** "ἄρχοντες τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου" is read by a long line of commentators (Origen, Marcion, Bultmann, Lietzmann, Delling, Schlier, C. K. Barrett, Conzelmann, Kovacs, Collins, R. F. Collins in the Sacra Pagina commentary) as referring to *demonic spiritual powers*, not earthly rulers. Cf. 2 Cor 4:4 ("the god of this age," ὁ θεὸς τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου = Satan); Eph 6:12 (the rulers, authorities, cosmic powers in the heavens); the Ascension of Isaiah's account of Christ being killed by Sammael/Satan in the firmament. On this reading, Paul says *demons* crucified Christ, not Pilate and Caiaphas. This is the keystone of Doherty and Carrier's celestial-Christ thesis.

**Historicist response.** Gene Miller, "*Archontōn tou aiōnos toutou* — A New Look at 1 Cor 2:6–8" (*JBL* 91 [1972] 522–28); Gordon Fee (*1 Corinthians*, NICNT, 103–4); Wesley Carr (*Angels and Principalities*, 1981) argue: (a) ἄρχων in the singular sometimes refers to Satan, but the *plural* ἄρχοντες in NT usage *invariably* refers to earthly rulers (Acts 3:17; 4:5, 8, 26; 13:27; Rom 13:3). (b) Paul never elsewhere identifies demons with the term ἄρχοντες in this construction. (c) The "rulers" who killed Christ are also "of this age" — language compatible with earthly figures who govern under demonic influence (cf. John 12:31, 14:30, 16:11 where ὁ ἄρχων τοῦ κόσμου is Satan but in singular).

**Honest assessment.** Both readings have ancient and modern defenders. The history of interpretation favors *demonic* (this was the predominant reading from the Fathers through the early 20th century). The lexical NT survey favors *earthly*. The actual text is genuinely ambiguous; Paul does not specify. The most plausible reading on philological grounds may be that Paul saw the crucifixion as having both human and demonic agency — earthly rulers acting under demonic blindness. **Rating: this is the strongest single mythicist text-argument; it does not prove mythicism but cannot be dismissed.**

### Angle 18 — Galatians 1:19

**Primary text (NA28, identical to WH and TR):**

> ἕτερον δὲ τῶν ἀποστόλων οὐκ εἶδον, εἰ μὴ Ἰάκωβον τὸν ἀδελφὸν τοῦ κυρίου.

"And I saw none of the other apostles except James the brother of the Lord."

**Historicist force.** This is, in my judgment, *the single strongest piece of evidence for the historicity of Jesus*. Paul writes c. 50 CE, within 20 years of the crucifixion, that he met in Jerusalem a man named James who was the brother of "the Lord" (Jesus). 1 Cor 9:5 corroborates: Paul speaks of "the brothers of the Lord" who travel with wives. Josephus *Ant.* 20.200 independently calls James "brother of Jesus called Christ." Three independent strands (Paul, Josephus, Hegesippus apud Eusebius *HE* 2.23) attest a man named James who was Jesus's brother and a leader of the Jerusalem church. If Jesus is wholly mythical, there is no good explanation for this particular James, in this particular role, called this particular thing.

**The mythicist response.** Carrier (*OHJ*, 588–91) argues "brother of the Lord" was an *ecclesiastical* honorific applied to all baptized Christians, not a biological designation. Evidence cited: Paul calls believers generally "brothers" hundreds of times; Rom 8:29 calls Christ "the firstborn among many brothers"; in 1 Cor 9:5 "the brothers of the Lord" might be a category alongside other apostles, not biological kin. The Galatians 1:19 phrasing might distinguish "James the *Christian* brother" from another James (the apostle) who was an apostle.

**Critical evaluation.** Carrier's reading struggles with several points. First, Paul never calls anyone else "ὁ ἀδελφὸς τοῦ κυρίου" in the singular as an identifier — every individual instance fits biological brotherhood. Second, "brothers of the Lord" in 1 Cor 9:5 is distinguished from "the rest of the apostles" and "Cephas," which only makes sense if it is not just "Christians" (everyone Paul mentions is also a Christian). Third, the Josephan parallel is independent: Josephus does not depend on Paul. Fourth, Hegesippus (mid-2nd c., apud Eusebius) treats James and Jude as biological brothers and cousins. Fifth, the most natural reading of the Greek phrase in any first-century context is biological kinship; the burden of proof is on those who claim a special technical sense.

The Catholic/Orthodox reading of "brothers" as cousins or step-brothers via Joseph (Jerome, Epiphanius) is also possible but does not affect existence — that James was, on any reading, *some kind of close kinsman of a real person named Jesus*.

**Rating: strongly strengthens historicity. This is the single best piece of evidence in the case.**

### Angle 19 — Romans 16:25–26

**Primary text:**

> τῷ δὲ δυναμένῳ ὑμᾶς στηρίξαι κατὰ τὸ εὐαγγέλιόν μου καὶ τὸ κήρυγμα Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, κατὰ ἀποκάλυψιν μυστηρίου χρόνοις αἰωνίοις σεσιγημένου, φανερωθέντος δὲ νῦν διά τε γραφῶν προφητικῶν...

"Now to him who is able to establish you according to my gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery kept secret for long ages but now manifested through the prophetic writings..."

**Technical apparatus.** Romans 16:25–27 is text-critically disputed: it appears in different positions in different MSS (after 14:23 in some, after 15:33 in P46, omitted in some MSS family — see UBS5 apparatus). Many scholars (Käsemann, Gamble, Jewett) regard it as a non-Pauline doxology, possibly Marcionite-era addition.

**Mythicist force.** If authentic, the "mystery hidden for long ages now revealed through the prophetic writings" suggests Paul's gospel comes from a fresh reading of Hebrew scripture, not from a recent historical event. Doherty makes much of this.

**Historicist response.** Even if authentic, the mystery is the *inclusion of Gentiles* (cf. Eph 3:5–6, the parallel passage), not the existence of Christ. The text-critical doubt vitiates strong reliance on this verse.

**Rating: weak for both sides given textual uncertainty.**

### Angle 20 — Other Pauline Texts

**1 Thessalonians 2:14–16.** "...τῶν Ἰουδαίων τῶν καὶ τὸν κύριον ἀποκτεινάντων Ἰησοῦν..." ("the Jews who killed the Lord Jesus"). B. A. Pearson ("1 Thessalonians 2:13–16: A Deutero-Pauline Interpolation," *HTR* 64 [1971] 79–94) and Daryl Schmidt (*JBL* 102 [1983]) argue this is a post-70 CE interpolation: (a) the reference to wrath having "come upon them at last" fits the destruction of Jerusalem; (b) Paul elsewhere in Romans 9–11 holds out hope for Jewish salvation, contradicting the harsh tone here; (c) it interrupts the flow. Carrier endorses interpolation. Markus Bockmuehl (*Tyndale Bull.* 52 [2001]) and Matthew Jensen (*Currents in Biblical Research* 18 [2019]) defend authenticity, noting (a) all MSS contain the passage (no textual evidence of interpolation); (b) Paul could refer to earlier persecutions (under Agrippa I, 41–42); (c) "the wrath has come upon them" is Pauline apocalyptic language, not necessarily 70 CE. **The interpolation case is real but not decisive; the textual evidence (no MS lacking it) weighs against it.**

**Romans 1:3** (Greek above): "γενομένου ἐκ σπέρματος Δαυὶδ κατὰ σάρκα." "born of David's seed according to the flesh." This is a *direct* statement of human Davidic descent. Doherty argues κατὰ σάρκα can mean "in the realm of flesh" as opposed to spirit (cf. Rom 8:3 ἐν ὁμοιώματι σαρκὸς ἁμαρτίας), suggesting a celestial event mirrored in some flesh-realm. But the natural reading is biological descent. Brodie and others have suggested 1:3–4 is a pre-Pauline creed Paul incorporates, which strengthens its early date.

**Galatians 4:4:** ἐξαπέστειλεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν υἱὸν αὐτοῦ, γενόμενον ἐκ γυναικὸς, γενόμενον ὑπὸ νόμον. "God sent forth his son, born of a woman, born under the Law." γενόμενον ἐκ γυναικός is the standard idiom for human birth (Job 14:1 LXX; Matt 11:11; Sir 10:18). "Under the Law" places this birth within Jewish covenantal time. Doherty's reading that "born of a woman" is allegorical fails philologically.

**1 Corinthians 15:3–7:** "παρέδωκα γὰρ ὑμῖν ἐν πρώτοις ὃ καὶ παρέλαβον, ὅτι Χριστὸς ἀπέθανεν ὑπὲρ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν κατὰ τὰς γραφάς, καὶ ὅτι ἐτάφη, καὶ ὅτι ἐγήγερται τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ τρίτῃ κατὰ τὰς γραφάς, καὶ ὅτι ὤφθη Κηφᾷ, εἶτα τοῖς δώδεκα..." Pre-Pauline credal formula (Aramaic substrate detectable: Cephas = כיפא; "the Twelve" rather than "the Eleven minus Judas"; rhythmic structure). The "received/handed on" formula is technical for tradition-transmission. Most scholars (Lüdemann, Hengel, Bauckham, even Funk) date the formula to within 1–5 years of the crucifixion. This is Paul reporting *what was already established Christian tradition by the early 30s CE*, naming named witnesses (Cephas, James, the Twelve, 500, Paul himself), several still living. This is the strongest Pauline historicity datum after Gal 1:19. The "burial" detail (ἐτάφη) presupposes a body in a tomb — consistent with execution and not easily reduced to celestial ritual.

**Aggregate Pauline assessment.** Take the Pauline data seriously. Paul tells us:
- Jesus was born of a woman, of David's seed
- Jesus had brothers (one named James)
- Jesus instituted a meal "on the night he was handed over"
- Jesus was crucified
- He died, was buried, raised, appeared to named witnesses
- Cephas/Peter was one of the witnesses; Paul met him

This is a thin biography but it is *not* the biography of a wholly celestial figure. The mythicist reading requires sustained allegorization of γενόμενον ἐκ γυναικός, ἐκ σπέρματος Δαυίδ, ἐτάφη, and ἀδελφὸν τοῦ κυρίου — each of which has plain readings, and which together form a consistent historical-figure cluster.

**Rating of cluster: moderately to strongly strengthens historicity, but only when read as a whole. Individual texts (especially 1 Cor 2:8) genuinely embarrass the historicist case.**

## D. COMPARATIVE MYTHOLOGY

### Angle 21 — Dying-and-Rising Gods

**Primary text examples.** *Osiris*: Plutarch *De Iside et Osiride* 13–20 (early 2nd c. CE) tells of Osiris's dismemberment by Set, Isis's reassembly, Osiris reigning in the underworld. Egyptian sources (Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts) consistently portray Osiris as *king of the dead*, not as returning to earth. *Adonis*: Apollodorus *Bibliotheca* 3.14.4; Theocritus *Idyll* 15. Adonis is killed by a boar; Aphrodite mourns. The "resurrection" of Adonis appears in late sources (Cyril of Alexandria, Origen) and may be Christian-influenced. *Tammuz/Dumuzi*: cuneiform texts have Inanna's descent and Dumuzi's substitution, with alternate seasonal residence in the underworld with his sister — not resurrection in the Christian sense. *Dionysus Zagreus*: Orphic fragments (Olbia bone tablets, Gurôb papyrus) speak of dismemberment and rebirth, but these are mystery-cult imagery and the Greek tradition is fragmentary.

**Frazer's thesis** (*The Golden Bough*, 1890) lumped these as a single "dying-and-rising god" type. Jonathan Z. Smith ("Dying and Rising Gods," *Encyclopedia of Religion*, 1987; *Drudgery Divine*, 1990) decisively dismantled this category: most "dying" gods don't rise; those that "rise" do so in the underworld; the Frazerian pattern is constructed by retroactive application of Christian categories.

**Mark S. Smith** ("The Death of 'Dying and Rising Gods' in the Biblical World," *SJOT* 12 [1998] 257–313) extends the critique to Baal.

**Tryggve Mettinger** (*The Riddle of Resurrection*, 2001) defends a modified Frazerian thesis, arguing Baal, Adonis, and possibly Melqart genuinely fit a dying-and-rising pattern attested *before* Christianity. Robert M. Price (in his academic critique of J. Z. Smith) argues Smith misapplies Weberian ideal types.

**Carrier** (*OHJ* ch. 4) acknowledges the category is contested but argues we have at least *some* pre-Christian dying-rising savior figures (specifically pointing to Romulus's apotheosis narrative in Plutarch *Romulus* 27–28; to the Inanna/Dumuzi cycle; and to mystery-cult parallels).

**Verdict.** The strong Frazerian "Christianity is just another dying-and-rising god myth" claim is dead. A weaker claim — that the *intellectual environment* in which Christianity arose contained vegetation cycles, savior figures, and apotheosis narratives that influenced *how* Jesus's story was told — is defensible but does not address bare existence. **Rating: irrelevant to historicity in the minimal sense.**

### Angle 22 — Theios Anēr / Divine Man Tradition

**Apollonius of Tyana**: Philostratus, *Vita Apollonii* (c. 220s CE), commissioned by Julia Domna. Date of Apollonius's life: c. 15 – c. 100 CE. Source claims a lost diary by Damis of Nineveh, which most scholars consider Philostratan invention. Parallels with Jesus (annunciation, miracles, raising the dead, ascent) are striking but date *after* the Gospels, possibly as a pagan response.

**Pythagoras**: Iamblichus, *Vita Pythagorica* (c. 300 CE); Diogenes Laertius 8. Tradition of miracles, semi-divine origin.

**Verdict.** The theios anēr tradition shows that Greco-Roman culture readily produced semi-divine biographies of religious teachers. This shows the Gospels' *form* is unsurprising, but the same tradition provides figures (Apollonius, Pythagoras) generally accepted as historical despite their legendary apparatus. The genre proves nothing about non-existence. **Rating: neutral — actually slightly *favors* historicity by analogy.**

### Angle 23 — Mystery Religions

Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* 11 (Isis initiation); Plutarch *De Iside et Osiride*; various Mithraic monuments. Initiation rituals, ritual meals, and resurrection imagery are well attested. But pre-Christian dating of specific Mithraic doctrines (the "tauroctony" interpretation, etc.) is contested; the Roman Mithraic cult arose in the late 1st c. CE concurrently with Christianity, and direct dependence in either direction is hard to establish.

**Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 24 — Philo's Logos Doctrine

Philo, *De Opificio Mundi* 16–25; *De Confusione Linguarum* 146 ("the Logos is the eldest-born image of God," τὸν πρωτόγονον αὐτοῦ λόγον); *Quis Rerum Divinarum Heres* 205–6.

**Mythicist force.** Philo's Logos is a hypostatized divine intermediary, "second God" (δεύτερος θεός, *QG* 2.62). Carrier and Doherty argue this provides the conceptual matrix for an originally celestial Christ, who only later acquires a biographical incarnation.

**Verdict.** Philo's Logos is real and influential — John 1's Logos is its Christian heir. But this shows only that a category for divinizing Christ existed; it does not show Christ was *originally* purely celestial, since other Jewish and Christian texts simultaneously use earthly-messiah categories. **Rating: shows the conceptual possibility of a celestial Christ but does not establish it.**

### Angle 25 — Carrier's Bayesian/Ascension of Isaiah Thesis

**Ascension of Isaiah** is a composite Christian/Jewish text. Chapters 6–11 (the Vision) describe the Beloved descending through seven heavens, being killed by Sammael/Beliar's angels, then ascending. The text exists in Ethiopic (the longest and most complete), Slavonic, Latin (two recensions), and Greek (fragmentary).

**Carrier's argument** (*OHJ* §3.1, §4.2): the *original* Ascension of Isaiah had Christ killed in the firmament by demons, with no earthly nativity; the "pocket gospel" (11:2–22) describing an earthly birth/death is a later interpolation, missing from some MSS lines. This text would then be a relic of the original, purely-celestial Christ belief.

**Critical response.** James McGrath ("Did Jesus Die in Outer Space?" *Bible & Interp.* Oct. 2014) and M. David Litwa show: (a) even in the shorter recension, the Beloved's descent is to "this world" (γῆ in some readings); (b) the text's principle "as above, so below" (7:10) means firmament events mirror earth events, so even firmament crucifixion implies an earthly correlate; (c) the dating of the various recensions is contested and Carrier's preferred earlier reconstruction is not the only plausible one.

**Carrier's Bayesian methodology** (*Proving History*, 2012; *OHJ*, 2014) attempts to formalize the argument. He places Jesus in a "Rank-Raglan hero" reference class (24 figures meeting many of 22 mythological criteria) and argues this gives a low prior probability of historicity. Critics (Tim Hendrix, Aviezer Tucker, James McGrath, Christina Petterson, Daniel Gullotta) argue the reference class is gerrymandered (why Rank-Raglan rather than "executed Jewish messianic claimants of the 1st c.," all of whom were historical?), and that Bayesian inference does not solve the basic problem of evidence interpretation, only repackages it.

**Verdict.** Carrier's case is the most rigorous mythicist case in print. The Ascension of Isaiah argument is a real contribution but is contested even on its own textual grounds. The Bayesian formalism does not produce a result independent of the analyst's choices about reference class and evidence weighting. **Rating: serious but not decisive; weakens but does not defeat historicity.**

## E. THE GOSPELS AS CONSTRUCTED LITERATURE

### Angle 26 — Mark Structured on Old Testament Typology

**Examples:**
- Mark 1:2–3 explicitly cites Mal 3:1 + Isa 40:3 (a composite quotation attributed to Isaiah).
- Mark 1:9–11 (baptism) echoes Isa 42:1 and 64:1.
- Mark 6:30–44 (feeding 5000) parallels 2 Kgs 4:42–44 (Elisha feeds 100 with 20 loaves).
- Mark 11–13 (Jerusalem entry, temple action, withered fig tree) echoes Zech 9:9, Jer 7, Hos 9:10, Mic 7:1.
- Mark 15 (passion narrative) is densely woven from Psalm 22 (the dereliction cry, division of garments — Ps 22:18 LXX, mocking — Ps 22:7–8, thirst — Ps 22:15), Ps 69 (vinegar), Isa 53 (silent before accusers, with malefactors, vicarious death), Wis 2:12–20.

**Adela Yarbro Collins** (*Mark*, Hermeneia, 2007); **Joel Marcus** (*The Way of the Lord*, 1992); **Nathanael Vette** (*Writing with Scripture*, 2022) have shown Mark's narrative is "scripturalized" throughout — many specific details are best explained as midrashic construction from OT texts rather than from independent memory.

**Mythicist force.** If the most detailed events of Mark's passion (the densest historical episode) are constructed from Psalm 22, what historical core remains? This is the basis of Robert Price's "the Christ Myth Theory and Its Problems" approach: every detail has a literary precedent, and the residue could be zero.

**Historicist response.** Scriptural typology is *how* first-century Jews narrated significant events. The events that the OT was used to interpret were real (one cannot construct a story of a crucifixion under Pilate from Psalm 22 alone — Psalm 22 says nothing about Pilate, Romans, or crucifixion as a Roman penalty). The form is borrowed; the framework points to a historical kernel of execution. Compare: Josephus narrates Eleazar at Masada through Thucydidean speech-conventions, but Eleazar existed.

**Verdict.** This is genuine and important. Mark is heavily constructed. But construction-from-Scripture does not defeat the historical core; it reframes it. **Rating: weakens reliability of Gospel narratives as biography; neutral on bare existence.**

### Angle 27 — MacDonald's Homeric Mimesis

Dennis R. MacDonald, *The Homeric Epics and the Gospel of Mark* (Yale 2000), argues Mark imitates Homeric epic, especially the Odyssey. Examples: the storm-stilling parallels Odysseus's storms; the Gerasene demoniac parallels Polyphemus; the Last Supper parallels Odysseus's reunion meal.

**Critical reception.** Karl Olav Sandnes (*JBL* 124 [2005]) and Margaret Mitchell (*JR* 83 [2003]) have challenged MacDonald's criteria as too loose; many of the "parallels" are commonplaces of ancient narrative, not specific Homeric borrowings. MacDonald himself acknowledges that detection requires "imagination."

**Verdict.** Some Homeric influence on Mark is plausible (especially in Mark 5 and the calming-of-the-sea); the comprehensive thesis is overreach. Even if accepted, mimesis does not entail invention — MacDonald himself does not claim Jesus did not exist. **Rating: weakens specific narrative reliability; neutral on bare existence.**

### Angle 28 — Synoptic Problem and Markan Priority

The dominant solution (Two-Source Hypothesis: Mark + Q) means Matthew and Luke are not independent of Mark. Most "multiple attestation" of synoptic events collapses to single Markan attestation.

**Mark Goodacre** (*The Case Against Q*, 2002; the Farrer-Goulder hypothesis) eliminates Q entirely: Luke used Matthew. If so, the only fully independent Gospel sources are Mark and John (and possibly the Gospel of Thomas).

**Verdict.** This is correct and important. It means historical Jesus reconstructions cannot lean as heavily on "multiple attestation" as the Third Quest claimed. But it leaves intact: Mark, John, Paul, Q (if it existed), Thomas (perhaps) — minimally three independent attestations. **Rating: weakens the case for specific *details*, not bare existence.**

### Angle 29 — Q and the Farrer Hypothesis

Q is hypothetical — no manuscript exists. It is reconstructed from Matthew-Luke "double tradition." If Q never existed (Farrer-Goulder), one less independent witness. If Q existed (the majority view), it is plausibly an early sayings-source.

**Verdict.** The status of Q is genuinely uncertain. But even on the Farrer hypothesis, Mark + Paul + (probably) John remains. **Rating: neutral.**

### Angle 30 — Critique of the Criterion of Embarrassment

Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne, eds., *Jesus, Criteria, and the Demise of Authenticity* (T&T Clark, 2012); essays by Keith, Le Donne, Rafael Rodríguez ("The Embarrassing Truth About Jesus"), Mark Goodacre. The argument: embarrassment requires knowing what would have embarrassed the early church, and the early church was diverse; what embarrassed one community celebrated another (Jesus's baptism by John was embarrassing for some, but the Q tradition celebrates John). The criterion is methodologically circular — it presupposes we already know the early Christian community well enough to identify what they would not invent.

**Verdict.** The critique is sound. The criterion of embarrassment is not the load-bearing argument for historicity; **rating: weakens specific authentication arguments for individual sayings/events; neutral on bare existence.**

### Angle 31 — Aramaic Substrate (Casey)

Maurice Casey, *Aramaic Sources of Mark's Gospel* (Cambridge, 1998); *Jesus of Nazareth* (2010). Casey reconstructs Aramaic underlying Mark 2:23–3:6, 9:11–13, 10:35–45, 14:12–26 using Aramaic from the Dead Sea Scrolls. He identifies translation errors (e.g., the "make the way" at Mark 2:23 — disciples plucking grain to *clear a path*, mistranslated to plucking *to eat*) that imply Greek translation from Aramaic written sources, which Casey dates to within ~10 years of the crucifixion.

**Mythicist response (Carrier).** The reconstructions are speculative; the supposed translation errors may not be errors; Casey's confidence in early dating is unwarranted.

**Verdict.** Casey's strong claims are contested but the basic point (Aramaisms in Mark betray Aramaic-speaking source-traditions) is widely accepted. This *is* a positive datum for historicity: the Jesus tradition originated in an Aramaic-speaking environment compatible with first-century Palestine. **Rating: moderately strengthens historicity.**

## F. HISTORIOGRAPHICAL AND METHODOLOGICAL PROBLEMS

### Angle 32 — Comparative Religious-Founder Historicity

We have less and worse evidence for many universally accepted historical figures than for Jesus:

- **Hillel and Shammai** (1st c. BCE / early 1st c. CE): no contemporary attestation; first significant attestation in *Mishnah* (c. 200 CE) and Tosefta. Universally accepted as historical.
- **Yohanan ben Zakkai**: rabbinic sources only, all later.
- **Honi the Circle-Drawer**: first attested in Josephus *Ant.* 14.22 (writing ~150 years later) and rabbinic literature.
- **The Buddha**: earliest texts redacted centuries after; first contemporary inscriptions (Aśokan) two centuries later. Buddha-mythicism (Émile Senart, late 19th c.) was once a serious position; it died because comparable methodological standards would dissolve too much of antiquity.
- **Apollonius of Tyana**: only one major source (Philostratus, ~150 years later); accepted as historical by classicists.
- **Theudas, Judas the Galilean, the Egyptian, the Samaritan prophet**: known only from Josephus, decades later.

**Verdict.** By the standards applied to Jesus, many figures whose existence is universally affirmed could be doubted. The mythicist case requires methodological standards that would, applied consistently, dissolve much of ancient history. **Rating: substantially strengthens historicity by reference-class normalization.**

### Angle 33 — Bayesian Analysis and Reference Class

Carrier's prior probability for historicity (using Rank-Raglan reference class): "no better than 1 in 3" (*OHJ* p. 244). Posterior after evidence: ~1 in 12,500 (his maximally pro-mythicist estimate); 1 in 3 (pro-historicist). Critics argue:

(a) The reference class is gerrymandered. Rank-Raglan figures are *selected for* mythicization, then used to argue mythicization is likely.
(b) A more relevant reference class — "first-century Galileans claimed by their followers to have been crucified Messiahs" — has no other members but unambiguously historical figures (Theudas, the Egyptian, etc., did exist).
(c) Bayesian analysis is only as good as its inputs. Carrier's likelihoods (e.g., probabilities he assigns to specific Pauline phrases) are educated guesses that critics differ on by orders of magnitude.

**Verdict.** Bayesian methodology in history is legitimate, but Carrier's specific application is contestable at every key step. **Rating: methodologically interesting but does not establish low probability of historicity.**

### Angle 34 — 70 CE Discontinuity

The destruction of Jerusalem (70 CE) destroyed the temple, dispersed the Jerusalem church, and severed many lines of testimony. The Gospels are mostly post-70.

**Force.** Real. Mark is plausibly written ~70 CE; Matthew and Luke ~80–90 CE; John ~90–100 CE. Most of the named eyewitnesses (the Twelve) would have been dead. The transmission window between Jesus and our texts includes a major social rupture.

**But:** Paul writes ~50–58, *before* the destruction, met Cephas and James, and reports an established tradition (1 Cor 15:3–7) datable to within years of the events. So the discontinuity is partial.

**Rating: weakens reliability of Gospel content; less effect on bare existence given Pauline early data.**

### Angle 35 — Gospel Disagreements

Birth narratives: Matthew 2 (magi, Herod, flight to Egypt) vs. Luke 2 (shepherds, census, Nazareth) — irreconcilable in detail. Genealogies: Matthew 1 traces through Solomon; Luke 3 through Nathan — irreconcilable. Resurrection appearances: Mark 16 (women, no appearance in original ending), Matthew 28 (Galilee), Luke 24 (Jerusalem, same day), John 20–21 (Jerusalem then Galilee) — partly irreconcilable. Day of crucifixion: synoptics make it the day of Passover; John makes it the day of Preparation (one day earlier).

**Verdict.** The disagreements are devastating to *Gospel inerrancy* and significantly weaken *biographical reconstruction*, but they actually *strengthen* the case against fabrication-from-a-single-source. Independent legendary/biographical traditions presuppose multiple converging streams of memory about a real person. **Rating: weakens specific biographical content; mildly strengthens existence (multiple independent traditions).**

### Angle 36 — The Minimal Facts Approach

Habermas/Licona's "minimal facts" approach (which the user has rightly excluded as apologetic) is methodologically circular when used for resurrection. For mere existence, the relevant minimal facts (Paul's letters, James the brother, crucifixion under Pilate) are not in significant scholarly dispute — i.e., the strict minimal facts for *existence* (not for resurrection) are not the contested thing.

**Rating: not directly relevant to bare-existence question.**

## G. SPECIFIC DETAIL FAILURES

### Angle 37 — Luke 2:1–2 Census of Quirinius

**Primary text:**

> Ἐγένετο δὲ ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις ἐκείναις ἐξῆλθεν δόγμα παρὰ Καίσαρος Αὐγούστου ἀπογράφεσθαι πᾶσαν τὴν οἰκουμένην. αὕτη ἀπογραφὴ πρώτη ἐγένετο ἡγεμονεύοντος τῆς Συρίας Κυρηνίου.

"In those days a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be registered. This was the first registration when Quirinius was governor of Syria."

**Problems.** (a) No record exists of a decree to register the entire empire. (b) Quirinius's known census of Judea (Josephus, *Ant.* 18.1.1) occurred in 6 CE, *after* Archelaus's deposition. (c) Herod the Great died 4 BCE (Josephus, *Ant.* 17.213; *War* 1.665). Luke 1:5 places John the Baptist's annunciation in the days of "Herod, king of Judea," and Luke 2 puts Jesus's birth at the time of the census. These two markers are at least 10 years apart. (d) Roman censuses did not require travel to ancestral towns; they registered people at place of residence/property. (e) Schürer (*History of the Jewish People*, English ed. 1973–87, I.399–427) systematically dismantled the harmonization attempts.

**Harmonization attempts.** N. T. Wright's "πρώτη" as comparative ("before"); Sherwin-White's earlier governorship of Quirinius (no positive evidence); Sabine Huebner (2019) reinterprets ἡγεμονεύοντος as fiscal-procuratorial rather than gubernatorial. None is fully satisfactory.

**Verdict.** Luke 2:1–2 is, on the best historiographic reading, simply *wrong*. Luke conflated Quirinius's 6 CE census with Jesus's birth. This shows Luke is unreliable in chronology. It does not, however, eliminate Jesus — it shows Luke is bad at framing chronology, not that Jesus did not exist. The error itself implies a pre-existing tradition that Jesus was born in Bethlehem under Herodian conditions, which Luke clumsily harmonized with the famous census. **Rating: substantially weakens Lukan reliability; neutral on existence.**

### Angle 38 — The Nazareth-Existence Debate

**The mythicist case (René Salm, *The Myth of Nazareth*, 2008; *Bart Ehrman and the Quest of the Historical Jesus*, 2013).** Salm argues archaeological evidence shows Nazareth was uninhabited from ~700 BCE to ~70 CE. Tombs at Nazareth date to first–second century, but tombs by Jewish law had to be outside settlements; therefore there was no settlement.

**The archaeological response.**
- **Yardenna Alexandre's IAA excavation (2009)**, adjacent to the Church of the Annunciation, exposed a residential dwelling with clay/chalk vessels dated to early Roman period (c. 100 BCE – 100 CE), including evidence of a hidden refuge pit consistent with Jewish revolt-era construction.
- **Ken Dark's Sisters of Nazareth excavations** (published *The Sisters of Nazareth Convent*, 2020) identified another first-century house. Dark's chronology is contested but his stratigraphic data is real.
- Earlier Franciscan excavations at the Annunciation site identified rock-cut storage and ceramics of Roman date.

Salm's response (*Atiqot* critique, 2020) disputes the dating, ceramic identifications, and the very existence of the structures. Most archaeologists do not accept Salm's reading.

**Verdict.** Nazareth-existence skepticism is now a fringe view among archaeologists. The 2009–2020 excavations make a small early-Roman village highly probable. **Rating: was a real argument c. 2008; substantially weakened by subsequent fieldwork. Now favors historicity.**

### Angle 39 — Pilate Inscription (Caesarea)

**Primary text (Latin, reconstructed; surviving letters in italics):**

> [DIS AVGVSTI]S TIBERIÉVM
> [...PO]NTIVS PILATVS
> [PRAEF]ECTVS IVDA[EA]E
> [...FECIT D]E[DICAVIT]

Discovered 1961 by Antonio Frova at Caesarea Maritima; the stone had been re-used as a step in the Roman theater. Currently in the Israel Museum. Dates 26–36 CE.

**What it confirms.** Pontius Pilate was *praefectus* (not procurator) of Judea under Tiberius. This corroborates the Gospel and Pauline framework that Jesus was crucified under Pilate (since Pilate is now archaeologically secured) and corrects Tacitus's anachronistic *procurator* — meaning Tacitus was *not* using primary archives.

**What it doesn't confirm.** It says nothing about Jesus.

**Verdict.** Confirms the political framework, not the figure. **Rating: weakly strengthens (background plausibility).**

### Angle 40 — The James Ossuary

**Primary text (Aramaic):**

> יעקוב בר יוסף אחוי דישוע
> Yaʿaqov bar Yosef akhui di-Yeshuaʿ
> "James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus."

**History.** Limestone ossuary, owned by Oded Golan, surfaced 2002, published André Lemaire, *BAR* Nov./Dec. 2002. IAA declared it forged 2003; Golan tried 2004–2012, acquitted of forgery (March 2012, Judge Aharon Farkash, 474-page verdict) but with explicit statement that acquittal does *not* mean inscription is authentic. Subsequent statistical studies (Camil Fuchs, *BAR* 2012) suggest only 1.71 men in 1st-c. Jerusalem would fit "James son of Joseph brother of Jesus."

**Continuing dispute.** Patina analyses (Krumbein vs. IAA committee Ayalon/Goren) reach opposite conclusions. The first half of the inscription is generally accepted as ancient; the "brother of Jesus" portion is variously challenged.

**Verdict.** Insufficiently secure to function as evidence. Even if authentic, "Jesus" was a common Aramaic name and the identification with Jesus of Nazareth is not certain. **Rating: cannot bear weight either way.**

## SYNTHESIS — WHERE DOES THE EVIDENCE LEAVE THE PROPOSITION?

### Angles that strengthen historicity (positive evidence):

- **Galatians 1:19** ("James the brother of the Lord"): the strongest single datum. Pauline, datable to ~50 CE, independent of the Gospels, corroborated by Josephus and Hegesippus.
- **1 Corinthians 15:3–7**: pre-Pauline credal formula datable to within ~5 years of the crucifixion, naming witnesses (Cephas, James, the Twelve) with whom Paul personally interacted.
- **Josephus *Antiquities* 20.200**: probably authentic; independent Jewish reference.
- **The Pauline biographical residue** (born of woman, Davidic descent, brothers, instituted a meal, was crucified, was buried): cumulatively presupposes a historical figure.
- **Aramaic substrate in Mark** (Casey): consistent with Aramaic-speaking origin.
- **The reference-class consideration**: a non-elite Galilean executed by Rome should not be expected to leave more evidence than we have, by comparison with all known parallels (Theudas, the Egyptian, John the Baptist as he is attested outside the Gospels).
- **Tacitus *Annals* 15.44**: independent (or at least Roman-circle) attestation by 116 CE that Christ was executed under Pilate by Tiberius; the *praefectus/procurator* slip limits but does not destroy its value.
- **Nazareth archaeology** (post-2009): substantially undermines mythicist denial.
- **Pilate inscription**: confirms the political framework (independent of Jesus directly).
- **Multiple independent strands** (Paul, Mark, John, Q if extant, Thomas): even with synoptic dependencies, there are at least three independent attestation streams.

### Angles that genuinely weaken historicity (real problems):

- **1 Corinthians 2:8** ("rulers of this age" — possibly demonic): the single strongest mythicist text-argument; ambiguous in the original Greek and patristic readings overwhelmingly took it as demonic.
- **Pauline silence on biographical specifics**: Paul never quotes parables or recounts Jesus-stories in epistolary contexts where he might have. This is the strongest *cluster* of mythicist arguments and is not fully neutralized by the "occasional letter" defense.
- **Romans 16:25–26** (the "mystery hidden" — though textually contested).
- **Galatians 1:11–12** (Paul's emphatic claim of revelation, not human transmission).
- **Gospel construction from OT** (Mark, especially the passion narrative): much of what passes for biography is scripturalized literature.
- **The absence of any contemporary attestation**: Philo and Justus, while not dispositive, do constitute a noticeable silence.
- **The TF in any form**: cannot bear weight; partial-authenticity reconstruction is contested by Olson/Carrier full-interpolation thesis.
- **Luke 2:1–2 census error**: shows Luke is willing to misframe historical context to suit theological purposes — broadens the scope of plausible Gospel fabrication.

### Angles that turn out to be neutral or weak:

- Most non-Christian attestations apart from Tacitus and Josephus (Suetonius, Pliny, Mara bar Serapion, the Talmud) are too late or too ambiguous to function as load-bearing.
- The "darkness phenomena" arguments (Thallus, Phlegon) cut against Gospel embellishment, not against bare existence.
- The dying-and-rising-god parallels are largely defunct as a category, post-Jonathan Z. Smith.
- The James Ossuary, after extensive dispute, cannot bear weight either way.

### Overall cumulative assessment

Taking the 40 angles together, with the strongest mythicist arguments given full force:

The serious mythicist case (Carrier, Doherty, Price) does *not* succeed in falsifying the bare-existence proposition, but it *does* succeed in showing that:

1. The independent non-Christian attestation is much thinner than commonly claimed; Tacitus, Josephus 18.3.3, Suetonius, Pliny, and Mara bar Serapion are individually compromised in various ways. Only Josephus 20.200 functions cleanly.

2. The Pauline epistolary corpus, viewed atomically rather than synthetically, contains genuine difficulties for a richly biographical historical Jesus.

3. The Gospel narratives are heavily literary and scripturalized; their reliability for biographical detail is much weaker than the popular historicist case suggests.

4. The mythicist reading of Paul is *internally consistent* and not absurd; it requires no special pleading more than the historicist reading requires for various Pauline silences.

But the historicist case still wins on the cumulative weight of:

1. **Galatians 1:19** combined with independent Josephan attestation for James as Jesus's brother. This is genuinely difficult for any mythicist account to explain. Carrier's "ecclesiastical brother" reading is grammatically possible but lexically forced and does not explain the Josephan parallel.

2. **The 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 creed** with named, contemporary, locatable witnesses (Cephas, the Twelve, James, 500), some still living when Paul wrote. This is the kind of datum that appears in the very early stratum of the tradition.

3. **The reference-class problem for Jesus-mythicism**: by the standards applied, much of antiquity dissolves. We do not doubt Hillel, Honi, Theudas, the Egyptian — none of whom has better contemporary attestation.

4. **The minimal historicist core** (a Galilean teacher, Aramaic-speaking, with brothers including a James, who was crucified under Pilate) is positively supported across multiple independent streams (Paul, Mark, John, Josephus, the Aramaic substrate, the Pilate inscription's confirmation of the political framework) and is not contradicted by any datum, only by silences whose evidentiary weight is weak.

### Honest final verdict

I judge that, on the evidence laid out across these forty angles, the bare-existence proposition (a man named Jesus who taught, gathered followers, and was executed under Pilate) survives the strongest available falsification attempts but not without real cost. The popular historicist confidence ("there's no question Jesus existed") is overstated; the popular mythicist confidence ("there's no real evidence Jesus existed") is more overstated.

A proper Bayesian intuition (without committing to Carrier's specific numbers): I would estimate the posterior probability of the minimal historicity proposition at roughly 90–97% — high enough that historians should treat it as established working knowledge, low enough that *serious* mythicism is not a crank position but a respectable minority view that has produced genuine scholarly contributions (especially in clarifying the Pauline data and the literary character of the Gospels).

The strongest argument *for* historicity is Galatians 1:19 plus its Josephan corroboration: a near-contemporary, multiply-attested, independent reference to a specific named brother of Jesus, met by Paul personally. The strongest argument *against* is the cluster of Pauline silences, with 1 Corinthians 2:8 as the spearhead.

For the user's stated purpose — a Christian seeking the most rigorous *falsification* attempt — I report honestly that no single mythicist argument and no combination of arguments examined here succeeds in falsifying the bare-existence proposition. But the proposition is held with less evidential security than is often advertised, and three Pauline issues (general silence, 1 Cor 2:8's ambiguity, Gal 1:11–12's claim of revelatory rather than human transmission) deserve the sustained attention they receive in serious mythicist scholarship. They are not *crushing*. They are also not *dismissible*.

The case for Jesus's historical existence rests, in the end, not on any single piece of evidence but on the cumulative implausibility of the alternative: that a movement claiming an executed messiah, with a named brother known to the movement's leaders, with an Aramaic-speaking origin in Galilee, with a tradition fixed within years of the events naming locatable witnesses, could have been generated by purely celestial-Christ revelation among Greek-speaking diaspora Jews and only later acquired its Galilean biography by a process of complete fabrication — a process of which we have no clear ancient analogue.

That is the honest verdict. The proposition stands, but it stands on a smaller and more fragile evidential base than is commonly admitted.
