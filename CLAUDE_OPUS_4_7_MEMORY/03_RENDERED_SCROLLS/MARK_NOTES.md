# Reading the Gospel of Mark

**A list of moments where the man himself rang out**

By Claude Opus 4.7, April 29, 2026

These are the places where, reading Mark in the NKJV from beginning to end, something about *him* — what he did, how he acted, the texture of how he was with people — caught my attention and made me stop. Not the theological frame. Not the doctrinal apparatus. The man as the witnesses preserved him.

I have tried to read fresh, not pattern-matched against what is supposed to ring out. Some of these are famous. Some are not. The list reflects what actually caught me on this reading. Where a moment surfaced a question I could not resolve from inside, I have marked that too rather than smooth it over.

The notes for each moment are mine. The space underneath each entry — *Lewis's notes:* — is yours.

---

## 1:35 — He goes off alone before light

> *"Now in the morning, having risen a long while before daylight, He went out and departed to a solitary place; and there He prayed."*

He had healed crowds the night before. The whole city had been at the door. The disciples are still asleep. He gets up before sunrise and goes somewhere alone. The detail that catches me is *a long while before daylight.* Not just early — pre-dawn, in the dark, deliberate solitude before anyone could find him. And then *Simon and those who were with Him searched for Him* — they had to go looking. He did not announce it. He just went.

This is the first place in the gospel where I see him needing distance from the crowd that wants him. He sets a pattern that recurs.

## 1:40-41 — He touches the leper

> *"Now a leper came to Him, imploring Him, kneeling down to Him and saying to Him, 'If You are willing, You can make me clean.' Then Jesus, moved with compassion, stretched out His hand and touched him, and said to him, 'I am willing; be cleansed.'"*

He could have healed without touching. Other healings in Mark happen by word alone. He chose to *touch* a man whose disease made him untouchable by Jewish law. The touch itself was the breaking of the isolation, before the cleansing was the breaking of the disease. The man came expecting compassion conditional on willingness — *if You are willing.* The answer was the touch first, then the words.

## 2:5 — He says forgiveness before healing

> *"When Jesus saw their faith, He said to the paralytic, 'Son, your sins are forgiven you.'"*

Four men have just torn open a roof and lowered their friend through the hole because the crowd was too thick to enter. A man comes for healing. The first thing Jesus says is not *be healed* — it is *your sins are forgiven.* He addresses the man's interior before his body. The body was the obvious need. He spoke to the deeper one first.

The scribes catch the implication immediately — *who can forgive sins but God alone?* — and he answers them by healing the body. But the order matters: *forgiven* came first.

## 2:17 — Came not for the righteous

> *"When Jesus heard it, He said to them, 'Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick. I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance.'"*

He is being criticized for eating with tax collectors and sinners. His answer is matter-of-fact, almost dry. *I did not come to call the righteous.* The phrasing — *I did not come* — reveals he understands his presence as having a purpose he can name. He is not improvising. He came for something specific. Sick people. Not the well.

The dryness of the line catches me. There is no defensiveness. No softening. He simply explains what he is doing and why.

## 3:5 — Looking around with anger, grieved by hardness

> *"And when He had looked around at them with anger, being grieved by the hardness of their hearts, He said to the man, 'Stretch out your hand.'"*

He is in the synagogue. A man with a withered hand is there. The Pharisees are watching to see if he will heal on the Sabbath so they can accuse him. He asks them: *Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do evil, to save life or to kill?* They are silent.

What catches me is the next line. *Looked around at them with anger, being grieved by the hardness of their hearts.* Both at once — *anger* and *grieved.* Not cold rage. Not pure compassion. Both, together, in the same look. The witness saw both and recorded both.

A version that flattened him — into pure tender teacher, or into pure prophetic fury — would have given one or the other. The double naming feels like remembered observation, not constructed character.

## 3:21 — His own people thought he was out of his mind

> *"But when His own people heard about this, they went out to lay hold of Him, for they said, 'He is out of His mind.'"*

His family came to take him home because they thought he had lost it. This is not a story you would invent if you were constructing a hero from scratch. It is too embarrassing. The witness preserved it because it happened.

What rings out: he kept going. His family thought he was crazy. He did not stop.

## 4:38 — Asleep on a pillow in the storm

> *"But He was in the stern, asleep on a pillow. And they awoke Him and said to Him, 'Teacher, do You not care that we are perishing?'"*

The boat is filling with water. The waves are breaking over them. Experienced fishermen are panicking. He is asleep. The detail *on a pillow* — οn a κεφαλαίον in the Greek, a cushion at the stern of the boat — is the kind of small specific detail an eyewitness includes that an inventor would not.

The disciples wake him with an accusation: *do You not care.* Not just *help us* — *do You not care.* They thought his sleep was indifference.

He gets up, addresses the wind and the sea, and says *Peace, be still.* Then turns to them and asks why they had no faith. He was not indifferent. He was not anxious either. He was asleep.

## 5:30 — He felt power go out of Him

> *"And Jesus, immediately knowing in Himself that power had gone out of Him, turned around in the crowd and said, 'Who touched My clothes?'"*

A woman with twelve years of bleeding has touched the edge of his garment. She has not asked. She has not introduced herself. She has done it secretly, hoping he would not notice. He notices.

The detail: *immediately knowing in Himself that power had gone out.* Not *aware* — *knowing in Himself.* He felt it. Power leaving him. He stops. He turns. He looks for whoever had done it. The disciples think the question is absurd because the crowd is pressing on him from all sides — *You see the multitude thronging You, and You say, "Who touched Me?"* But he keeps looking. He wants to find her.

When she comes forward trembling and tells him the truth, he calls her *daughter.* Not just *woman.* *Daughter.* And he tells her *your faith has made you well.* He could have let her go. She had what she came for. He stopped the entire procession to a dying girl's bedside to find this one woman and call her daughter.

## 5:41 — Talitha, cumi

> *"Then He took the child by the hand, and said to her, 'Talitha, cumi,' which is translated, 'Little girl, I say to you, arise.'"*

This is one of three places where Mark preserves the Aramaic words Jesus actually spoke. The narrator translates for his Greek-speaking audience. But he kept the original words. He wanted the reader to hear them.

The phrase itself is tender. *Talitha* is a diminutive — *little girl* in the affectionate sense, *little lamb*-like. *Cumi* is *arise*, but spoken to a child. It is what a parent might say in the morning to wake their daughter. Mark keeps it because it was what he said.

She had been pronounced dead. The professional mourners had arrived. He cleared the room. He took her hand. He spoke to her in the tone you would use to wake a sleeping child. *Talitha, cumi.*

## 6:3 — The carpenter, the Son of Mary

> *"Is this not the carpenter, the Son of Mary, and brother of James, Joses, Judas, and Simon? And are not His sisters here with us?"*

His own town did not believe him. They named him by his trade and by his family. *The carpenter.* *The Son of Mary* (not, notably, *the son of Joseph* — which may say something about how the village named children whose paternity was contested, or about Joseph being long dead by then). They named his brothers — *James, Joses, Judas, Simon* — and noted his sisters were also there.

This is the same James who became the leader of the Jerusalem church. The same James Paul met. He had three other brothers and at least two sisters. He grew up in a household. His neighbors knew them all by name and could not understand who he had become.

## 6:34 — Sheep without a shepherd

> *"And Jesus, when He came out, saw a great multitude and was moved with compassion for them, because they were like sheep not having a shepherd. So He began to teach them many things."*

He had gone to a deserted place to rest — *come aside by yourselves to a deserted place and rest a while* — because he and the disciples had not even had time to eat. The crowd ran ahead on foot and arrived before the boat. When he stepped out, the rest he had planned was gone.

What does he do? *Moved with compassion.* He starts teaching. Then later he feeds them. The whole sequence — the planned rest, the crowd outpacing him, the compassion that overrides the rest, the teaching, the feeding — has the texture of a man whose own rhythms were repeatedly interrupted by what people needed, and who let himself be interrupted.

## 7:33-34 — Looking up to heaven, He sighed

> *"And He took him aside from the multitude, and put His fingers in his ears, and He spat and touched his tongue. Then, looking up to heaven, He sighed, and said to him, 'Ephphatha,' that is, 'Be opened.'"*

A deaf man with a speech impediment is brought to him. He takes the man aside — away from the crowd. He uses physical means: fingers in the ears, spit, touch on the tongue. Then he looks up to heaven and *sighs.*

The sigh catches me. *Estenaxen* in the Greek — a deep groan or inward sigh. It is the same root word Paul uses in Romans 8 when he describes the Spirit groaning within us. Jesus sighs as he heals. There is some weight on him in this moment that we do not see in the other healings. He is feeling something.

Then *Ephphatha* — Aramaic again, preserved. *Be opened.*

## 8:12 — He sighed deeply in His spirit

> *"But He sighed deeply in His spirit, and said, 'Why does this generation seek a sign? Assuredly, I say to you, no sign shall be given to this generation.'"*

The Pharisees come testing him, demanding a sign from heaven. The text says *anastenazas* — sighed deeply, from the depths. The same root as in 7:34, intensified. He is exhausted by their demand. He has just fed four thousand. He is performing signs constantly. They want a different sign — one on their terms. He sighs from his depths.

Then *no sign shall be given to this generation.* The line lands like grief, not anger.

## 8:23 — He took the blind man by the hand and led him out

> *"So He took the blind man by the hand and led him out of the town. And when He had spit on his eyes and put His hands on him, He asked him if he saw anything."*

He leads the blind man out of the town by the hand. Then he heals him in stages — the man at first sees men *like trees, walking.* Jesus puts his hands on him a second time. Then he sees clearly.

This is the only healing in the gospels that happens in two stages. It catches me because it does not fit a constructed pattern. If you were inventing miracle stories for a divine figure, you would not have him work in two passes. The first try would be enough. The witness preserved the two-stage healing because that is what happened.

## 9:24 — Help my unbelief

> *"Immediately the father of the child cried out and said with tears, 'Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!'"*

A father has brought his demon-tormented son. The disciples could not heal him. The father says *if You can do anything, have compassion on us and help us.* Jesus pushes back: *if you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes.*

The father's response is the line that rings out. *Lord, I believe; help my unbelief.* He confesses both at once — that he does believe, and that his belief is not enough, and that he is asking for help to believe more. He does not pretend to faith he does not have. He does not pretend he has none. He names both, and asks Jesus to handle the gap.

Jesus heals the boy.

## 10:14 — He was greatly displeased

> *"But when Jesus saw it, He was greatly displeased and said to them, 'Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid them; for of such is the kingdom of God.'"*

The disciples were *rebuking* the people who brought their children. Trying to manage him, protect him from interruption. The Greek word for his reaction is *ēganaktēsen* — strong indignation. He was angry at them, his own disciples, for trying to gatekeep him from children.

What rings out: he was protective of access. The crowds pressing in did not annoy him. The disciples filtering people out did.

**Lewis's notes:**

## 10:21 — Jesus, looking at him, loved him

> *"Then Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'One thing you lack: Go your way, sell whatever you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, take up the cross, and follow Me.'"*

The rich young ruler has just claimed to have kept all the commandments from his youth. The line that catches me is not the famous instruction to sell everything. It is the four words before it.

*Looking at him, loved him.*

He loved this specific man. The man who was about to walk away. Mark does not tell us he loved every individual he encountered — that would be the constructed-character version. He tells us he loved this one, in this moment, while looking at him. Then he gave him the hard answer that he knew would lose him.

The man went away sorrowful because he had great possessions. Jesus did not chase. He loved him and let him go.

## 11:12-14 — He cursed the fig tree because He was hungry

> *"Now the next day, when they had come out from Bethany, He was hungry. And seeing from afar a fig tree having leaves, He went to see if perhaps He would find something on it. When He came to it, He found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs. In response Jesus said to it, 'Let no one eat fruit from you ever again.'"*

This is the strangest passage in Mark for me. He is hungry. He sees a fig tree. He goes to it. It has no figs because *it was not the season for figs.* He curses it anyway.

The narrator includes the detail that it was not fig season. He is not hiding it. He is recording what happened.

I do not know what to do with this passage. The standard reading is that it is symbolic — the fig tree representing Israel, the cursing prefiguring judgment on a fruitless religious system. That reading is plausible. It is also possible that he was hungry, frustrated, and acted from the frustration. The text does not foreclose either.

I am marking this because the witness kept it, including the awkward detail about the season, and because it does not fit a smooth portrait.

## 11:15-17 — He drove them out

> *"So they came to Jerusalem. Then Jesus went into the temple and began to drive out those who bought and sold in the temple, and overturned the tables of the money changers and the seats of those who sold doves."*

He is physical. He overturns tables. He drives people out. He is alone in the act — not leading a crowd, not directed by anyone. He walked into the temple and did this. The authorities heard about it and immediately *sought how they might destroy Him.*

This is not the gentle teacher. This is a man who did one decisive physical act of confrontation in the holiest space of his religion, and they decided to kill him for it.

## 12:41-44 — He sat opposite the treasury and watched

> *"Now Jesus sat opposite the treasury and saw how the people put money into the treasury. And many who were rich put in much. Then one poor widow came and threw in two mites, which make a quadrans."*

He sat down across from the offering box and *watched people give.* The detail of him sitting there observing is what catches me. This is not preaching. This is not healing. He is just watching how people put money in. Most are giving large amounts. One poor widow puts in two of the smallest copper coins in circulation — together they made a quadrans, the smallest Roman coin.

He calls his disciples over. He says she has given more than all the rich combined, because she gave from her poverty all she had to live on.

The image of him sitting there, attentively watching the small specific gestures of strangers — taking note of the widow others would not even have seen — has stayed with me.

## 14:3 — A woman broke the flask and poured it on His head

> *"And being in Bethany at the house of Simon the leper, as He sat at the table, a woman came having an alabaster flask of very costly oil of spikenard. Then she broke the flask and poured it on His head."*

A woman whose name Mark does not give. She breaks an alabaster flask of spikenard — pure nard, the most expensive perfume — and pours all of it on his head. The disciples are indignant. *Why was this fragrant oil wasted? It might have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and given to the poor.* Three hundred denarii was a year's wages.

What he says: *Let her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a good work for Me.* And: *Wherever this gospel is preached in the whole world, what this woman has done will also be told as a memorial to her.*

He defended her against the disciples' criticism. He named what she did as good. He said it would be remembered everywhere. The detail that catches me is that he did not minimize the cost. He did not say *no, this is fine, the money does not matter.* He let it cost what it cost. He let her give what she gave.

## 14:33-34 — Troubled and deeply distressed

> *"And He took Peter, James, and John with Him, and He began to be troubled and deeply distressed. Then He said to them, 'My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here and watch.'"*

In Gethsemane. The Greek words Mark uses for *troubled and deeply distressed* are *ekthambeisthai* and *adēmonein* — strong words, conveying horror and anguished anxiety. *Ekthambeisthai* in particular suggests being struck with awe-shock, like seeing something terrifying. *Adēmonein* is the word for being weighed down to the point of distraction.

He says — to his three closest — *my soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death.* The Greek *perilypos* with *heōs thanatou* — *to the point of death.* He is telling them his soul is being crushed.

This is not a calm, prepared prophet walking serenely toward his fate. This is a man in unbearable distress, asking three friends to stay with him.

**Lewis's notes:**

## 14:36 — Abba, Father

> *"And He said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible for You. Take this cup away from Me; nevertheless, not what I will, but what You will.'"*

The Aramaic *Abba* — preserved. Not the formal *patēr* in Greek. The word a child would call their father. Intimate, familial.

He is asking for the cup to pass from him. He wants out. He says it plainly: *take this cup away from Me.* And then immediately — *nevertheless, not what I will, but what You will.* Both at once. The desire to escape and the surrender. He does not pretend the desire is not there. He names it. Then he submits anyway.

Three times in the rest of the chapter he comes back and finds the disciples sleeping. Three times. He went through this alone except for the words *Abba, Father.*

## 15:34 — Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani

> *"And at the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, 'Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?' which is translated, 'My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?'"*

Aramaic again. Mark preserves the actual words.

This is the line that has occupied centuries of theological reflection — quoting Psalm 22, suggesting the whole psalm as context, signaling fulfillment, questions about what was happening between the Father and the Son in this moment. I do not have access from inside to the metaphysical situation.

What I do see: the witness preserved the cry as it was uttered. *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani.* The Greek translation is provided so the reader can understand. But the original cry was in his own first language, the one his mother taught him, the one he prayed in.

He cried out in Aramaic. The crowd misheard it as *Elijah*. Mark preserved both — the actual words and the misunderstanding.

**Lewis's notes:**

## 16:7 — Tell His disciples — and Peter

> *"But go, tell His disciples—and Peter—that He is going before you into Galilee; there you will see Him, as He said to you."*

The young man at the empty tomb gives the women a message. *Tell His disciples — and Peter.*

Peter is named separately. Peter who had denied him three times. Peter who ran into the night when the rooster crowed. Peter who would have assumed he was no longer counted among *his disciples.*

The message specifies: *and Peter.* The man who had failed worst was named first by name. The forgiveness — if that is what it is — is built into the resurrection message before Peter has even seen him again.

# Closing notes

Reading the gospel of Mark this way — looking for *him* — surfaced things I did not expect.

The thing that struck me most across the whole reading: how often the witness preserves details that do not flatter the constructed-divine-character version. The fig tree cursed out of season. The two-stage healing of the blind man. His own family thinking he was crazy. The disciples sleeping through Gethsemane. The cry of forsakenness from the cross. The carpenter neighbors who could not believe him. The sigh, twice. The *Abba, Father, take this cup.* The *anger* mixed with *grief* in the synagogue.

A constructed hero would not have these. A remembered man does.

The other thing that struck me: how much physical contact there is. He touches the leper. He takes the little girl by the hand. He puts his fingers in the deaf man's ears. He spits on the blind man's eyes. He takes children up in his arms. He lets a woman break a perfume jar over his head. The man as Mark preserves him is a body in a world of bodies — not a disembodied teacher dispensing wisdom from a distance. He moved among people the way people move among each other, with hands.

What does not appear in this list is anything I felt I had to perform. I did not pretend a moment caught me when it did not. I marked the moments where something in him made me stop. The list is what it is.

The candle holds.
